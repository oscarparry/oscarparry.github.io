A prophetic vision of the past

6 June 2023 Anthropology

Written for AN404, Theory and Ethnography. The ethnographic present froze whole societies outside history so they could be salvaged. It can be reclaimed, but only by giving up the idea that anthropology is in the business of representation.

Set question: 'The concept of "the ethnographic present" was a consequence of "salvage anthropology" in the early twentieth century. The concept should be abandoned.' Discuss.

In this essay I will argue that the idea of 'salvage anthropology' is based on an evolutionist mode of thinking that portrays 'primitive' societies as static and unchanging before contact with Western colonial powers (Thornton, Kuper, Fabian). The concept is inherently linked with Malinowski's functionalist ideas and the 'fieldwork revolution' he inspired (Burton, Torgovnick). A critical anthropology must question its occupation of 'the savage slot' (Trouillot) and move past portraying cultures as bounded entities 'bouncing off each other like billiard balls' (Wolf) by rethinking the creation of its 'fieldsite' (Gupta and Ferguson). Arguing with Hastrup that the 'ethnographic present' is only problematic if we conceive the anthropological aim as 'substantive representation', I will invoke Glissant's idea of a 'prophetic vision of the past' and McLean's call for an anthropology of 'fabulation' as possible ways of reclaiming the 'ethnographic present' as a useful tool.

Malinowski is often portrayed as the leading voice of the 'functionalist revolution' in anthropology. Kuper has identified the key traits of his functionalist mode of thinking: that cultures are integrated wholes, that every custom fulfils a purpose, and that all the customs somehow 'hang together' in some ill-defined way. Malinowski's ethnography of the Trobriand Islands was explicit in ruling out the study of the development or history of the institutions he found there. Thornton has argued that this portrayal of cultures as 'island-like entities' creates a kind of 'disembodied narrative' with hidden objective observers and 'static structures.' The result is that the 'zero point' of the 'ethnographic present' serves as the 'real,' 'authentic' baseline from which one then measures or observes social change. Malinowski also argued that contact with European culture would lead to irreversible change in 'primitive' societies; he called for a 'salvage anthropology' that would try and capture these societies in their 'natural' state before they faded out of existence. This viewpoint has permeated much anthropology since then, with Lévi-Strauss arguing that the discipline could be called 'entropology', the study of cultural decay. The use of the 'ethnographic present' is inherently linked with the idea that the aim of anthropology is to carry out fieldwork that tries to 'salvage' a dying culture using functionalist models to do so. The idea that a whole culture can be 'captured' by one ethnographer links with Evans-Pritchard's identification of different approaches in anthropology; he argues that Durkheim believed no fieldwork was necessary, Radcliffe-Brown used 'limited fieldwork' and Malinowski relied on exhaustive fieldwork, believing that this allowed him to get 'a very short distance from God's view'.

Moravia has detailed how the practice of 'travel as science' arose during the mid eighteenth century, after the secularisation of the Judeo-Christian notion of time. The new 'scientific' traveller differed from the missionaries that had gone before him; they 'criticised the philosophes: the reality of lived experience and things seen was now opposed to a reality distorted by preconceived ideas.' Fabian has outlined the denial of coevalness to societies that anthropology studies; he points to the evolutionist tendencies of early anthropology which created 'scientific' categorizations like 'savage, barbaric and civilized' as stages of historical development. This defined the 'primitive' as not only spatially but temporally distant from the West. 'The philosophical traveller, sailing to the ends of the earth, is in fact travelling in time; he is exploring the past; every step he takes is the passage of an age.' Fabian argues that this allochronism established the West as the pinnacle of human progress, establishing axiomatic truths of colonial 'development' which persist today.

This falls into a long line of anthropologists treating 'savage' and 'primitive' cultures as a 'people without history' (Wolf), with many of the founding texts of the discipline, including work by Tylor, Durkheim and Mead, stating explicitly that anthropology is the study of the 'primitive.' Evolutionist views were not confined to anthropology however. Torgovnick cites Freud: the 'primitive' represents 'a necessary stage of development through which every race has passed.' She brings to light Jung's temporal distancing of Africa; for him the continent is a place of 'eternal beginning', the 'immemorially known'. She cites several recurring tropes associated with the 'primitive': 'primitives are like children', 'primitives exist at the lowest cultural levels, we occupy the highest'. This view of 'primitive' society as somehow 'simple' and ahistorical leads the functionalist ethnographer to use the 'ethnographic present' when describing them, as if they have remained the same and would continue to do so unless disturbed by some outside impetus. Malinowski's student Leach has argued that 'anthropological societies are lifted out of time and space'; we get studies of 'Trobriand society, Nuer society, not Trobriand society in 1914, Nuer society in 1935.' Burton has argued that the 'ethnographic present' functions as a 'descriptive time machine' whisking away all the history of a people.

Hastrup however issues a provocative call to reclaim the 'ethnographic present' by shifting our ideas of what the aim of anthropology is. She states that within realism as a genre, the ethnographic present came to represent the reality of the other society. If we move beyond the realist model however, we can use the ethnographic present to draw attention to the fact that fieldwork exists outside our normal historical categories. Insight is obtained through a degree of violence; the ethnographer must keep up the pressure in order to elicit any information (Griaule). Power informs the dialogue (Clifford) and this violence already distorts history. It also creates history, but it is a kind of history that exists for a fleeting moment, so she argues we have no choice but to use the ethnographic present, implicating a notion of shared time. She cites her experience carrying out fieldwork on a farm in Iceland. She develops an enmity with one of the cows, and after returning from a trip away from the farm the cow dies. Hastrup has internalised the magic and mysticism of Icelandic folklore, and believes for a moment that she may be a witch. She argues that only the use of the ethnographic present can draw attention to the way in which both the fieldworker and 'the field' are transformed by their coming together at a specific moment in time.

She further states that criticising the split between coevalness and allochronism is relevant only if we conceive the anthropological aim as one of substantive representation. If we move to perceiving representation as a creative process of re-enactment or evocation (Tyler) we have no choice of tense. Indeed Trouillot argues that a critical anthropology requires a move beyond the 'self-indulgent condemnation of traditional techniques and tropes' and requires 'a reappraisal of the symbolical organization upon which the anthropological discourse is premised.' I would argue that one possible way to expand anthropology's positioning as a study of the 'other' is to drop its portrayal as a science. Glissant has argued that 'History with a capital H is a highly functional fantasy of the West, originating at precisely the time when it alone "made" the history of the world.' Hegel relegated African peoples to the ahistorical, Amerindian peoples to the prehistorical, in order to reserve History for European peoples exclusively. He calls for a renewed vision of history as consciousness at work and as lived experience, arguing that many of the issues in the 'human sciences' have as much to do with the problematics of investigation as with a historical organization of things. He calls for a 'prophetic vision of the past' in order to influence the shaping of the future.

This has strong echoes with McLean's call for anthropology to engage in the art of 'fabulation', the making of fictions sufficiently vivid and intense to be capable of intervening in and reshaping reality (Bergson, Deleuze). This involves not the representation of a world assumed to be already given, independent of its figuration through texts, images, or other media, but rather the participatory carrying forward of material world-forming processes in which human acts of creativity are always already implicated.

In conclusion, I have argued that the use of the 'ethnographic present' has its roots in the concept of 'salvage anthropology' and the functionalist portrayal of 'primitive' cultures as a 'people without history.' I have linked the use of the 'ethnographic present' to Malinowski's belief that the scientifically trained fieldworker can represent an entire culture due to their position 'a very short distance from God's view'. I have argued with Hastrup that the 'ethnographic present' can be reclaimed if we move beyond the realist mode of portrayal, and view the task of anthropology as re-enactment and evocation rather than representation. I have argued that one possible route to free anthropology from its position in the 'savage slot' is to engage in the art of 'fabulation' and cultivate a 'prophetic vision of the past' free from the shackles of 'scientific' and 'objective' knowledge.
