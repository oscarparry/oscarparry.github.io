Every view is a view from somewhere

1 June 2023 Anthropology

Written for AN404, Theory and Ethnography. Anthropology's claim to objectivity rested on a distance between the knower and the known. Taking that distance apart is not a literary problem but a political one.

Set question: Evaluate the statement that anthropological experiments with modes of knowing and writing "have sharpened our awareness of the charged nature of claims to objectivity and the situatedness of knowledge" (Abu-Lughod).

In this essay I will argue that an early 'scientific' anthropology that claimed to be in the business of finding Durkheimian 'social facts' has been thoroughly criticised for its lack of inquiry into its own architecture of knowledge production. The 'objective limits of objectivity' (Bourdieu) and the separation of nature and culture (Latour) must be investigated. Claims to objectivity are predicated on a separation of self and other which must be called into question. The positionality of the 'knowing subject' (Abu-Lughod) and the dialogical nature of anthropological knowledge production must be presented both in text (Clifford) but also 'on the ground' (Fabian).

Outlining a brief history of ethnography, it has its roots in the secularisation of science and the separation of the reality of experience from the philosophes; separated from preconceived ideas, the new 'scientific traveller' (Moravia) was different from the missionaries who had gone before them. The new ethnography was construed as impartial and objective, a necessary distance was created between the self and the 'other', the known and the knower. The claim to objectivity was predicated on the frank abstraction of a socio-political structure from the individual in any given context. The ethnographer was tasked with finding out those Durkheimian social facts that were self-evidently waiting to be discovered. The paradigmatic example of the scientific professional is Evans-Pritchard, who states that if he is accused of finding facts to fit his social theories, he has been understood.

However these claims to scientific objectivity have been subject to much criticism in recent years. Bourdieu identifies the 'objective limits of objectivity' in his theory of practice. He defines three modes of knowledge production: the phenomenological, the knowledge of everyday experience; the objectivist, the knowledge of wider social structures; and the theory of practice, which investigates the limits of objective knowledge. He argues that objectivist knowledge production by its very nature cannot investigate the architecture of its own production. Bourdieu states that 'scientific' knowledge of society requires not just an epistemological break with the knowledge of everyday life, but also a social separation from that everyday experience. Anthropology's 'knowing subject' is an outsider, condemned to be Husserl's 'impartial spectator' and forced into a hermeneutic representation of life by their lack of skin in the game of everyday life. He makes the analogy of comparing culture to a map: an outsider searching for directions makes a map of all possible routes, lacking the local knowledge which would allow them to easily find their way.

Bourdieu also identifies the 'fallacies of the rule.' He argues that anthropology has unquestioningly borrowed a Saussurean construction from linguistics. Saussure constituted the object of language from the speech act to allow certain grammatical rules to be established; this has been transplanted into anthropology with the separation of culture from conduct. Bourdieu argues that what is needed is an investigation into the dialectical relationship between objectivist structures and how they are actualised in the 'practice' of individuals in society.

Abu-Lughod builds upon Bourdieu's critique of objectivist knowledge by arguing that the very notion of the separation of the self and other should be called into question. She cites Strathern's definition of anthropology as a subject that makes 'collective representations' of an 'other,' which are fashioned by an 'unmarked self.' She highlights two groups who problematise the distinction between self and other: feminists and 'halfies.' Both of these groups call into question the distancing inherent in an objectivist anthropology. Feminist theory has learnt the danger of assuming a 'self' that is held in common by all women. Harding states that once women has been deconstructed into woman and gender is found to have no fixed referents, feminism has lost its ability to assume the voice of a naturalised or essentialised speaker. Strathern feels that there is a tension between feminist and anthropological modes of knowing; Abu-Lughod places this tension in the different ways the two subjects constitute the self, from the opposite sides of a power divide. Abu-Lughod states that anthropology needs to investigate the position of the self and recognise that the subject is part of a project of European expansion, that anthropology's 'will to knowledge' is inherently tied up with the colonial project. She states that no less than the 'halfie', the 'wholie' is speaking from a position within a wider social context, as a Frenchman in Algeria during the war of independence for example. Clifford writes that anthropology can only provide partial truths; Abu-Lughod argues that we must recognise that they are positioned truths as well: 'every view is a view from somewhere and every statement a speaking from somewhere.'

Further challenges to the claims of objectivity come from Bruno Latour, with his investigations into how science creates its object of study. He argues that a belief in 'modernity' is tied up with the separation of three objects: science, society and discourse. Latour argues that this separation disguises the proliferation of 'hybrids' that cross these dividing lines, and that an investigation of the 'networks' that are spread over the many academic disciplines will lead us away from an obsession with 'objective' knowledge. He calls for an investigation of the 'actors' in the network and how they mobilise objects, both human and non-human, to achieve their aims. This approach refuses to take the 'social' as a given. Rosenblatt states that there are deeply held Western ideas of what society is, rooted in the Book of Genesis and the idea of a 'social contract.' Latour asks what would happen if we did not assume that these ideas were true. He argues that any reference to 'social context' is the work of 'lazy sociologists' and argues that the specific mobilisations of a situation must be identified.

This echoes Abu-Lughod's call for an 'anthropology of the particular.' She argues that we must refuse to generalise in our writing; instead of making statements like 'the Nuer believe ...' we could look at how institutions are lived by individuals, for example how a family she works with in Egypt live the institution we call polygyny. This refusal to generalise will have three effects. First it will expose the construction of 'ideal types' used in much social science. Second it will draw attention to the particularity of every life, and how much of life's meaning comes from relationships with real individuals. Third it can counter the 'othering' discourses of social sciences and NGO agencies. This is an example of what she calls 'tactical humanism,' an emphasis on the shared experiences of individuals the world over, even though the construction of selfhood is very different, which can be deployed to emphasise global connections.

I would argue that anthropological experiments with modes of writing that highlight the dialogical nature of anthropology are to be praised. Clifford writes that much anthropology only includes fieldwork explications in order to establish 'ethnographic authority' before launching straight back into the 'all seeing eye' of representation. He cites Geertz as a paradigmatic example, including a short reference to arriving in a Balinese village and being ignored by the villagers, a police raid establishing rapport between Geertz and his interlocutors. Geertz then completely disappears from the ethnography, and a full picture of a cockfight with all its symbolic meanings explained is presented. The gathering of meanings from conversations with individual people disappears and Geertz presents himself as reading the culture 'over the shoulders' of 'the Balinese.' This highlights what Fabian finds to be the problem with 'ethnographic realism,' a surreptitious substitution of literary device for epistemological position. We must move towards an anthropology that is truly dialogical and does not assume a fixed 'culture' that can be 'read'.

Ethnographies that particularly highlight the dialogical nature of anthropology are Mintz's Worker in the Cane and Nash's We Eat the Mines and the Mines Eat Us. These two ethnographies are conducted in an interview style. Even though it can be argued that the ethnographer selects the questions and chooses what gets put into the final text, this literary style still highlights the way anthropological knowledge is collected. Worker in the Cane traces the life of Don Taso, a worker in Puerto Rico who spends his life in the cane field in back-breaking work. He joins the Socialist Party as a young man and is blacklisted from work, he has several children with his wife before falling seriously ill, and after a miraculous recovery he joins a Protestant revivalist sect. This portrait of a life problematises the notion of 'culture'; although larger forces like economics and religion affect Don Taso's life, the ethnography highlights his agency and search for meaning.

The very idea of the culture concept is homogenising and de-temporalizing. Anthropology must highlight the 'polyphonic' nature of any social group; Bakhtin's idea of a polyphonic text as a utopian space where different voices can make themselves heard is a goal to strive towards. But Fabian argues that a focus on genre could lead to anthropology relieving itself of transforming relations 'on the ground' through a 'literary therapy.' A move towards a historical anthropology, highlighting the 'structure of the conjuncture' of specific groups and the global connections between people, will take us with Wolf away from a notion of bounded cultures 'bouncing off each other like billiard balls.'

In conclusion, anthropology has thoroughly critiqued the notion of 'objective' knowledge, showing that it is predicated on the separation of the knower and the known. We must investigate how social forces act through individuals, the 'anthropology of the particular', and how actors mobilise the networks they are enmeshed in to achieve their aims. The refusal to generalise will highlight the constructed nature of much social science and help us identify our positionality with respect to other individuals. The dialogical nature of anthropology must be presented in the text, with a polyphonic ethnography as the ultimate aim. However the nature of relations 'on the ground' must be transformed as well. I argue with Trouillot that the symbolic order on which the discipline of anthropology is founded must be refashioned.
