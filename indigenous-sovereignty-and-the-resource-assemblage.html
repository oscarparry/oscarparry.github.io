Indigenous sovereignty and the resource assemblage

29 May 2023 AnthropologyLawEconomics

Written for AN479, Anthropology of Law. Sovereignty claims that look like a fight against the state often turn out to be a way of making land legible to capital, winning symbolic recognition while the political economy stays exactly where it was.

First published in coursework essay, MSc Social Anthropology, LSE

Written as exam practice on the AN479 topic of indigenous sovereignty.

In this essay I will argue with Goodale that indigenous claims to sovereignty, although achieving 'symbolic-political' (Scott) victories, cannot change the underlying political-economic power structure of a world-capitalist system (Wallerstein). I will argue that although indigenous sovereignty projects are often portrayed as being fought against the state, they are formed as much within the organizational terrain we call the state as in some wholly exterior space (Mitchell, Sieder, Abrams, Comaroff and Comaroff). Although Kirsch has identified counter-hegemonic possibilities in the use of international law for indigenous sovereignty, I will argue with Goodale that granting sovereignty is often used to create 'resource assemblages' that help to reduce the 'risky business' (Li) of investing in land commodification for transnational capital, illustrating this with ethnographic examples.

It is important to identify the genealogy of the concept of sovereignty in order to identify what philosophical underpinnings are introduced when it is mobilised in indigenous rights claims. The concept has its roots in the idea of the absolute and indivisible divine power of the sovereign to create laws over a particular territory, exercising a monopoly of violence over that space. Hobbes' covenant further developed the concept; he argued that 'the people' give up their right to be governed to their leader, in exchange for protection. However 'the people' is not a self-evident category and is constantly being negotiated. Contemporary theorists have argued that sovereignty is now 'unbundling' (Sassen) into supranational institutions like the WTO and the EU, with the introduction of environmental agreements, trade deals and the subjugation of many governments' control over their economy to the World Bank and the IMF. It is in this context that the debates over indigenous sovereignty must be interrogated.

Sieder's ethnography of the Quiché region of Guatemala analyses self-help justice projects as claims to sovereignty against a state that is often portrayed as 'failed' or 'weak'. She uses the example of three men who were killed by villagers after being accused of kidnapping a woman, with the police waiting helplessly outside the village. One of the village elders stated after the event that 'justice was done ... we know the police, the human rights and the courts are all corrupt.' The indigenous groups of the area are arguing that they need to introduce 'Mayan law,' a fusion of human rights discourse and 'customary' rules. However Sieder argues that although these sovereignty projects portray themselves as being opposed to the state, they depend on the state for recognition of their legitimacy. Abrams' seminal conceptualisation of 'the state' as both 'state system' and 'state effect' (Mitchell) illustrates how the ideological underpinnings of the state as a coherent and unified body shape the material reality of the state through the actions of individual actors who believe in this larger body. Mitchell argues that 'political subjects and their modes of resistance are formed as much within the organizational terrain we call the state ... as in some wholly exterior space.'

Indeed I would argue that far from being opposed to the state, granting indigenous sovereignty is often used to make indigenous leaders the state at the micro level. Research into the Nhlapo Commission, which aims to arbitrate chieftaincy claims in South Africa, illustrates how the use of 'customary' and 'traditional' authority is used as a blunt instrument to fashion new modes of government that have no resemblance to the more independent polity that existed before colonial rule. The weak conceptual underpinnings of the commission, using terms like 'a traditional leader is a leader who is traditional' and insisting on the use of male primogeniture to resolve dispute claims, leads to the assigning of chiefs in a way that is far from traditional. The hopeful chiefs often employ highly trained legal counsel who help them translate their claims into legal language, illustrating Kirsch's idea of the 'juridification' of indigenous politics, and the chiefs' control over the natural resources in their territory helps to set up a 'one-stop shop' for transnational capital investment in the area (Goodale).

I will argue that the granting of indigenous sovereignty has strong parallels with the system of 'indirect rule' set up by many colonial powers; the co-opting of existing power structures helps to make a potentially opaque system 'legible' for investment and resource extraction. Cohn has identified this in the Indian context, where the East India Company was given control over a huge area of Bengal. The directors of the company wanted to extract as much tax as they could from the area, but could not penetrate into the complex feudal land rights, relying on Indian interpreters to process claims for them. However their lack of trust in these interpreters eventually led them to take on the job themselves; they so badly misjudged how much tax they could extract that they caused one of the worst famines in history, in which around a third of the population of Bengal died (Davis).

Kirsch has argued that the use of international law for indigenous sovereignty claims is full of counter-hegemonic possibilities as both sides in a claim come to be involved in 'intercultural conversation' that has the potential to transform them both. He argues that these claims can lead to the 'horizontal exchange of ideas' between indigenous groups over the world, that they can influence jurisprudence, pointing to the establishment of 'subsistence rights.' He also argues that preparing for legal cases can drive social movements forward and result in the unification of otherwise antagonistic groups.

However, Goodale has illustrated how contemporary indigenous sovereignty claims are functioning as a means to create 'resource assemblages' for transnational capital investment. Research on the 1997 Indigenous Peoples' Rights Act in the Philippines, which covers around 80 per cent of the country's remaining natural resources, shows a process of governmentality called 'delineation,' resulting in an 'ancestral domain sustainable development and protection plan' (one of the most insidious names you could come up with), leading to land being exhaustively commodified and materialised. A similar process has been identified in the 2001 Cambodian Land Law, where communities must self-identify and even self-constitute in order to be recognised by the act, in ways similar to the Nhlapo Commission. In the twelve years after the law was passed, eight indigenous groups were recognised while over two million hectares of land were sold as 'economic land concessions' to 227 agribusiness corporations.

Goodale's main argument is that international law by its very nature serves as a tool to reduce risk for transnational capital investment. He argues that in contrast to Ferguson's claim that 'development' discourse functions as an 'anti-politics machine' by framing political problems as essentially technical, indigenous sovereignty discourse functions as a 'hyper-politics machine' by directing political claims towards 'symbolic-political' avenues rather than challenging the underlying 'political-economic' power structure of the world capitalist economy (Wallerstein). He points to ILO 107 and 169 as examples of the 'dramatic disconnect' between language couched in the markers of cultural recognition (Fraser) and the ILO functioning as a tool in the creation of controlled labour markets for capital (Standing). This process is illustrated by the work of the Special Rapporteur on the Rights of Indigenous People in Latin America. Although managing to influence the passing of 'soft markers' of justice, promoting cultural heritage and bilingual education, they were unable to facilitate 'hard markers' of justice like land redistribution and territorial self-determination.

In conclusion, I have argued that indigenous sovereignty claims, far from being opposed to the state, function as a means of achieving 'symbolic-political' victories that do not challenge the political-economic structure of the capitalist world economy that these states are linked to. I have argued that the setting up of these sovereign territories has parallels with the system of indirect rule and is often used to set up a system of government which is presented as 'traditional' but functions as a 'one-stop shop' for capital investment. The relentless 'delineation' of resources that often accompanies indigenous rights claims functions to create a 'resource assemblage' that reduces the risk for transnational capital investment.
