Law is governance, imposed

22 May 2023 AnthropologyLaw

Written for AN479, Anthropology of Law. If law is bound up with self-conscious projects of government, then calling every normative order a legal order loses sight of what makes law law, and of what it does when it arrives.

First published in coursework essay written under exam conditions, MSc Social Anthropology, LSE

Set question: "Law is centralised in its very nature ... Law is governance, imposed" (Roberts). Discuss.

In this essay I will argue with Roberts that the law is inherently tied up with self-conscious projects of government, and functions as a way of coding its power (Foucault). Although legal pluralists have contributed to viewing normative orders as made up of competing discourses, their portrayal of these as 'legal orders' effaces differences between them. I will illustrate the ideological portrayal of law as 'good' and its centralising effects through Walker's ethnography of the Urarina in the Amazon.

Roberts traces the genealogy of different approaches to conceptualising the law. In the early twentieth century, law was posed as inseparable from the state and its religious roots, with one early theorist stating 'there is the law of God and the law of the state. There is no other type of law.' Law was seen as inseparable from Judeo-Christian cosmology, deriving much of its power from the figure of the Judge and the divine sovereign, God's voice on Earth. Austin's influential theory of legal positivism separated law from morality, he viewed law as a set of commands emanating from a central authority, which did not necessarily have to have a grounding in religion as long as they achieved utilitarian success in their application.

These theories of law as inherently tied up with state power were challenged by the 'legal pluralists' who argued that a 'centrist ideology' was blinding many legal theorists to the reality of other normative orders. Sally Falk Moore's influential semi-autonomous social field argues that all methods of group control exhibit aspects of what Weber has termed a 'legal order.' Although the legal pluralists have made useful contributions to highlighting Teubner's 'suppressed discourses' and have highlighted how any normative order is made up of competing discourses, I will argue with Roberts that by calling these essentially negotiated orders 'law,' we begin to lose sight of what the 'law' actually is and may efface differences between law and other modes of normative order.

Roberts identifies three common ways that law is portrayed by the legal pluralists. First, the comparative legal theorist Sacco argues that 'law is everywhere.' Every society has some system of rules so therefore where there is society there is law. The second portrayal of law views it as a cosmology. Geertz argues that law is a system that allows people to imagine 'principled lives' they can lead; Roberts makes a case for this position being very similar to Durkheim's view of religion as a shared morality. The final vision of law finds its spokesperson in Pospíšil, who makes a case for law being derived from authoritative decision making. He argues that law arises when a Weberian 'legitimate authority' makes decisions, which happens in all societies.

The weakness of these positions is that they bundle law up with normative order, religion, and authority and government, meaning that we begin to lose sight of what makes law special. A challenge to Pospíšil's position comes from ethnographies of self-consciously acephalous societies like the !Kung of the Kalahari and Clastres' work in Amazonia; these ethnographies disrupt the Hobbesian notion that there needs to be an authority figure to keep the show on the road. In fact many of the issues with theorising law stem from a pernicious tendency to assume that a form of government is intrinsic to any social group. Roberts argues that if we do not assume this, we can make a connection between self-conscious projects of government and the formation of law, that law itself is a tool of governing.

Roberts states that wherever there is a sustained effort to take charge, to achieve Weber's organisation of society through Leiter and Verband, the formulation of structures and discursive formulations we call law will rise up. These include ritual processes, an ideological justification of power as some form of 'justice,' a portrayal of 'lawlessness' as chaotic and violent, the designation of autonomous zones under the overall power of a central authority, adjudicatory assemblages, a state monopoly on violence through a binary of legal and illegal violence, and what Foucault has termed the juridico-discursive coding of power relations. These processes are inherently centralising and can be found all over the world where self-conscious efforts of government have taken off, including imperial China and India as well as the West.

This ideological effect of 'law' as inherently good is one of the most powerful of its discursive strategies. It allowed law to function as the 'cutting edge of colonialism,' spreading 'civilisation' to countries that were portrayed as inherently violent. Pre-colonial regimes were often portrayed as 'despotic' in nature as Cohn has identified in the Indian context, while Comaroff and Comaroff have interrogated the meaning of claims of 'disorder' and corruption in the post-colony. I will argue that this view of law as somehow stabilising and non-violent is what allows government to be accepted. I will also argue that law recreates ideas of personhood and community which allow for centralising and governmental projects to be imposed.

Walker's ethnography of the Urarina in the Peruvian Amazon highlights both of these effects. Walker contrasts the use of law with the use of shamanism in dispute resolution, arguing that both are used in highly individualistic ways as a means of mobilising powerful agents to one's side in a dispute. He argues that mediation is often unachievable among the Urarina; their view of another person's mental state as inherently unknowable leads to a process of dispute where conciliation is almost impossible, so societal fission is a common occurrence, a dispute will lead to the two parties separating camp. However the imposition of the Peruvian government's law requiring Amazonian communities to settle in one place means that new mechanisms of dispute are being utilised. He points to extremely formal letters written to the local 'justice of the peace' in order to try and mobilise the power of state law onto one side of a dispute. However Walker argues that the difference between the use of law and the use of shamanic ritual is in its centralising effects. The old methods of dispute often led to societal fission, while the use of law cannot be controlled by the actors who invoke it, leading to the increased power of higher authorities in the local government.

Law is also remaking fundamental ideas of personhood and community in the Amazon. Morality is often bound up in the body, and also tied up with the division between human and non-human persons. In the past if an Amazonian committed a grave offence such as incest, no punishment would be carried out against them but they would be placed in the non-human category, with their personhood denied them. Now that law is being used by the villagers, there are conceptions of absolute right and wrong that are shared across society. The state's monopoly on violence is a major way that law distinguishes itself from other modes of dispute resolution. Walter Benjamin identifies the difference between the divine violence of the law and the mythic violence of the people; the divine violence of law expiates and establishes conceptions of justice outside of individual actors. I argue that this sense of shared morality creates what Benedict Anderson has termed an 'imagined community', a sense of shared connection between people that is essential to projects of government and the formation of a nation state. Walker identifies Deleuze's conception of 'state science' as remaking individuals as mini versions of the state, easily governable. The powerful ideological effects of the law which remake the past as a time of chaos and violence are also highlighted here. Walker shows how many of his interlocutors now view the past as incredibly violent, and state that they now have 'heaps of rights.' This links to Chanock's research into 'customary law' in Malawi, which is now viewed as incredibly violent. Strathern highlights the construction of a person as an individual with human rights in her ethnography of the 'coma girl' case in Papua New Guinea. There are competing visions of what it means to be a person in her ethnography, one that emphasises the divisible nature of a person and their relations and obligations with kinship groups, and one predicated on a Western legal definition of a person as a completely free individual, only bound by the higher laws of government.

In conclusion I have argued that a view of law as inherently linked to a project of government allows us to fully analyse its discursive and ideological effects and not confuse it with other normative orders that may function differently. I have argued that the legal pluralists' vision of law as everywhere, law without lawyers and legal documents, is a prime example of 'state science'; one of the ideological effects of the state is to see its effects everywhere, an inability to imagine societies without government. I have used Walker's ethnography of the Peruvian Amazon to highlight the effects of the imposition of law on a stateless society, how it remakes personhood and leads to centralising effects whatever the intent of actors who mobilise the law for themselves.
