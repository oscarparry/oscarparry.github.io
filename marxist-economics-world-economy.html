Marxist economics: the state of the world economy

26 May 2026 EconomicsChinaMarxismCrisis

A leadoff on the world economy. Everything that caused 2008 is still there, and four things have been added since: two major wars, a US-China trade war, protectionism, and sixteen years of low investment.

Written as a leadoff for a discussion on Marxist economics, and never published as an article. It is a speaking text, so it moves fast and leans on figures. Light copy-editing only.

What is the state of the world economy today?

All the ingredients of future crisis can be found in the world economy. The expanded role of finance capital, the short-sighted search for short-term profit. Debt dependency. Reliance on a series of bubbles, overvalued shares, houses, currency trading, commodities, emerging markets, to sustain growth.

That was written by Lynn Walsh in 2006, but it could easily be applied to the economy today, illustrating how none of the underlying factors that led to the 2008 crash have been resolved.

We can now add to that list two major wars, growing economic antagonisms along the US-China axis, the major pole of the world economy, a global rise in protectionism, increased environmental breakdown, a sixteen-year period of low growth and low investment, and record levels of inequality.

The main driver of world growth over the last few decades, China, is running into serious problems due to its lopsided growth, which was also driven by multiple bubbles: massive inflows of capital, over-investment in industrial capacity, a speculative housing boom, and colossal, debt-financed investment in infrastructure projects in the industrial regions.

All the conditions of a deep social and economic crisis are being prepared. This will not be a repetition of past crises. On the contrary, the capitalists will face crises in the next period that are likely to be even more devastating for their system, both economically and politically. The 'solutions' that were adopted to steady the ship after 2008 have sown the seeds for a future crash. More fundamentally, the factors which allowed the prolonged 'great moderation' period prior to 2007-08 have now definitely reached their limits. Probably future historians will look back and conclude that the 2007 crisis marked the end of that era, but it is only in the period now that the scale of change will become fully apparent.

We are entering a more brutal form of capitalism, shorn of more and more of the reformist cushioning of the post-war upswing. Under continuous assault by the capitalist class and disarmed by its own mass leaders, the working class has undoubtedly suffered setbacks and been weakened in terms of organisation and especially class consciousness. Nevertheless, the proletariat remains a powerful social force internationally and is actually being strengthened in some of the developing countries.

Socialist ideas are already attracting many freshly politicised young people, and they will win wider support in the next few years. Socialism, specifically Marxism, will become a serious competitor to capitalism. Why? Because Marxism is the only ideology capable of consistently expressing the interests of workers and other exploited and oppressed classes. It provides the theoretical tools for a fundamental critique of capitalism. Above all, it offers a programme for the abolition of capitalism.

Before 2008

I am not going to have time to go over the entire history of the capitalist economy, but it is important to touch on some of the elements of the buildup to the 2008 crash.

After the post-war boom came to an end, a period of stagflation in the 1970s was brutally suppressed through the 'Volcker shock', when the chairman of the US Federal Reserve massively raised interest rates, which reached almost 20% at one point. This led to what is known as the savings and loans crisis, in which many US banks that specialised in fixed term mortgages went bankrupt. The government intervened to reduce much of the regulation around mortgage lenders in an effort to restore profitability, which allowed a massive housing bubble to develop.

This was a small part of one of the most significant trends since the 1990s, the enormously increased role of finance capital, most dramatic in the US and other 'Anglo-Saxon' economies. In the US aggregate profits of financial corporations were about one-fifth as big as non-financial profits in the 1970s and 1980s. By 2000 they were more than half as big.

Profit levels in major OECD economies in the 1990s returned to the peak levels of the 1960s. Yet the growth of fixed capital stock was only half the level of the 1960s. More and more profits were channelled into financial speculation rather than productive investment. The international, short-term capital flows associated with speculation are extremely volatile, and increasingly threaten to destabilise economies. There were a spate of financial crises in the 1990s which all involved a US dollar peg unravelling, but none were big enough to destabilise the global economy.

Western, particularly US, capitalism restored profits by stepping up their offensive against the world's working class, due to the weakening of workers' consciousness and organisation in the post-Stalinist period, combined with the 1.2 billion additional workers added to the world capitalist economy. In the 'G7-plus' group of advanced capitalist countries real compensation as a share of gross domestic income fell from 56% in 2001 to a record low of 53.7% in 2006.

In Eastern Europe, within a decade of the collapse of the Soviet Union, around half of all Eastern European manufacturing capacity was in the hands of European multinationals. Motor vehicle production was 90% foreign owned. During the 1990s wages were less than a quarter of those in Germany. The Eastern European banking system was almost completely taken over by western owned banks, who by the end of 2008 had extended $1.3 trillion in credit.

China in particular played a major role in acting as an assembly plant for the west, with workers paid a tiny proportion of the wages of workers in the US or Europe. When China entered the WTO, the average wage of a Chinese car worker was 59 US cents an hour, less than 3% of a US car worker's wage. At the same time, the surpluses of the exporting countries, particularly China, underpinned the tide of liquidity. The Chinese state recycled parts of its trade surpluses, using its foreign exchange reserves to buy US government bonds on a vast scale in order to help to sustain the US economy and the ability of US workers to buy Chinese exports. Between 2002 and 2006, global liquidity increased by an estimated $3.9 trillion, over half of it from Asia, around 40% from oil producers as the price of oil skyrocketed.

The weakened bargaining position of workers and the deflation of prices through globalisation worked against inflationary pressures, at least in most advanced capitalist countries. Faced with potential crisis, the 2001 dotcom crash and the 9/11 shock, the central banks led by the US Federal Reserve slashed interest rates, which were negative in real terms for over 31 months after 2000. This injected massive liquidity into the world economy, producing new bubbles. These fed through to consumer demand, but at the same time produced an even higher mountain of debt.

Credit has been the fastest growing sector of the world economy since the 1980s. New types of financial instruments were invented, financial derivatives began to be developed. By 2005, their total nominal value represented three times total financial assets and ten times global GDP.

Inequality skyrocketed

The main activity of the investment banks and hedge funds that now dominate global capitalism is buying and selling debt between themselves, turning a profit on every transaction. This is undoubtedly a form of gambling, carried out at the expense of the great majority of society, that redistributes wealth from the majority to the rich, and from the rich to the super-rich. Between 1980 and 2005, the top tenth of taxpayers increased their share of taxable income from 34% to 44%. But the biggest gains were for the top tenth of 1% of the population, who increased their share of national cash income from 9% to 19%. The top one-hundredth of 1%, some 15,000 taxpayers, had an average income of $26 million after tax.

However, outside of China, the capitalists found it increasingly difficult to find profitable fields of investment in production. Despite the growth of new products, in many sectors there was, and remains, an overcapacity in relation to money-backed demand. Billions of people lack basic necessities, but they also lack the purchasing power to buy them. This over-accumulation of capital led to lower levels of capital investment, notwithstanding the enormous growth of the IT sector over the last four decades.

The annual growth of fixed capital stock, which takes account of the depreciation or obsolescence of worn out capital, in the United States fell from 4% in the 1960s to 3% in the 1990s and only 2% between 2000 and 2004. In Europe, the growth rate fell from 4.6% in the 1960s to 2.6% in 2000-04. Even more dramatically, the growth rate of fixed capital in Japan, seen as a super-accumulator during the post-war upswing, fell from 12.5% in the 1960s to 4% in the 1990s and 2.1% in 2000-04. China, of course, is an exception to this trend, with growth accelerating from 1.9% in the 1960s to 10.9% in the 1990s.

The 2008 crash

These factors were the buildup to 2008, and our party saw that it could well lead to a crisis. Particularly an article written by Lynn Walsh in December 2006, well before the crash began unfolding, which basically predicted the housing bubble unravelling. We often use the expression that Marxists do not have a crystal ball, but Lynn Walsh definitely got close to having one.

From the mid-1990s, the US developed an enormous housing bubble, fuelled by an abundance of cheap credit. Nationally, house prices rose 70% more than prices in general. As a result of the bubble, housing pumped an additional $5 trillion of extra paper wealth into the economy. Between 2001 and 2006, construction and related sectors, building materials, furniture, real estate finance and so on, accounted for 69% of private-sector job creation.

Many families used their homes like ATM machines, borrowing against rising value, 'cashing out' about $700 billion annually. In 2007 American consumers bought 16% of global output. It was not just a US phenomenon. Between 2001 and 2006, Greece, Finland, Sweden, Belgium, Denmark, the UK and France all experienced real estate booms more severe than in the US. In Ireland and Spain the boom was so big it would be enough to crash their economies completely when the crisis hit.

However, interest rate rises by the Federal Reserve, with the benchmark rate up from 1% in 2003 to 5.25% in 2006, made home loans more expensive and pushed house prices down.

After 2008

What were the consequences of the crash? World trade, the engine of globalisation, collapsed with a 16% fall in 2009. The United Nations in 2009 estimated that governments worldwide used around $18 trillion, about 30% of world gross product, to bail out the banks and support the financial system.

The US central bank provided $10 trillion to foreign central banks through currency swaps on top of $5 trillion in liquidity and loan guarantees to non-US banks.

According to the American think tank the Atlantic Council, the Federal Reserve, the Bank of Japan, the European Central Bank and the Bank of England have together used over $25 trillion of public money for quantitative easing. In the pandemic year alone, over $9 trillion was spent. In general, the quantity of money made available is the highest in history.

The Chinese regime responded in 2008 with a massive investment stimulus, and was able to do this because the state's control of its capital account meant that it could effectively depreciate its currency against the dollar. The US allowed this, but in doing so suffered a cumulative loss of about $4 trillion. At the start of the 2008 crisis, China's economy was 20% of the size of the US, now it is 60%.

Another factor preventing the complete breakdown of the financial system was the massive state stimulus from the Chinese government, which invested approximately $550 billion in society. Most of these investments went to the real estate industry and infrastructure projects that stimulated China's construction industry and drove urbanisation. However this massive investment has added to the instability of the Chinese economy, particularly driving a real estate bubble and an overproduction crisis due to China's limited domestic market. The consumption share of Chinese GDP was just 2% higher in 2019 than it was in 2007. This has led Chinese manufacturers to export their products, undercutting other countries' industries and leading them to adopt increasingly protectionist measures.

After 2016 in particular house prices in China became more and more unaffordable. The collapse of property giants like Evergrande in 2021 has led to an economic slowdown, and severe problems for the construction and property sector, which accounts for almost 30% of China's economic output, and which has battered economic growth recently.

The post-Covid lockdown economic rebound has been weaker than hoped, leading to the People's Bank of China starting to cut interest rates in order to attempt to encourage growth. Meanwhile youth unemployment has soared to over 20%.

Where China stands now

China's prolonged period of growth was unique in human history. From 1978 to 2019, GDP increased 60-fold. This was only possible with a unique set of circumstances, including the character of the state.

The Chinese state has been able to play a Bonapartist role, balancing between the classes, and it could do so because of the long period of growth over the last decades. That period is over. The perspective for the new multipolar era is for contradictions to accumulate in the Chinese economy, with decisive political effects, as capitalists and workers each press their own interests, within the country and within the CCP itself, a party of 100 million members.

In 2025 China recorded a trade surplus of $1.2 trillion, the largest in history, achieved even after Trump raised tariffs. Net exports made up roughly a third of China's GDP growth. An economy depending to that degree on external demand has aligned huge sections of its economy with markets it does not control, and the west is increasingly moving towards a trade war with China.

The so-called first China shock in the early 2000s fell on the United States, largely through low-value goods built with international capital invested in China. The second China shock falls more on Europe, and comes from higher-value production by Chinese-owned companies. The EU's goods deficit with China reached 360 billion euros last year, Germany's alone 95 billion. A French government report warns that entire sectors of European industry could collapse within a few years under this pressure. The German government is mapping Chinese vulnerabilities in preparation for a trade war, and Beijing has retaliated against EU sanctions.

However the western powers have not coordinated their response. That disorganisation may well persist, because of the competing interests of different western national capitalists.

Why can China not sell to its own people? The internal market was never developed to absorb what China produces, and the property slump has deepened the problem. House prices have fallen roughly 30% from their 2021 peak. Housing was one of the main stores of household wealth, and China has recorded its first fall in consumer spending since the pandemic, in the longest deflationary episode since the 1970s.

Chinese factories can build more than 55 million electric vehicles a year, but they use less than half that capacity. The result has been a price war of extraordinary ferocity. Last year the regime attempted to act, issuing guidelines banning carmakers from selling below their total cost of production. Despite warnings of severe penalties, the new year brought a fresh round of price cuts.

This is a classic crisis of overproduction. State investment called into being productive forces that have outgrown the market built to absorb them, and they are now hurled onto a world market where tariff walls are going up. The contradiction lies in the partial character of the planning: powerful enough to summon vast capacity into existence, unable to determine what becomes of it once the law of value asserts itself. And no national plan, however powerful, escapes the world market. This is the contradiction that Stalin's theory of socialism in one country can never resolve, and China is living it out on the largest scale in history.

There is a debt crisis, both in local government and nationally. Local provinces for a long period expropriated rural land cheaply and auctioned the use rights, supplying up to 40% of local revenue. That income is now less than half its 2021 peak. In 2025 local governments collected 12 trillion yuan and spent close to 24 trillion. They are spending twice what they raise. Total national revenue has fallen for the first time since 2020.

China has more than two million industrial robots installed, exceeding every other country combined. In Guangdong's industrial hubs, as many as two-thirds of workers at peak season are temporary. Gig work approaches 40% of urban employment.

Wage growth is at its lowest outside the pandemic and for the first time in decades the five-year plan has omitted an urban job creation target. Growth is concentrating into a few coastal technology hubs while the provinces that built the export boom fall behind: Guangdong, which if counted as a country would be the world's sixth largest exporter, has lagged the national growth rate for four years.

This is not a stable equilibrium. China Dissent Monitor recorded a 44% increase in protests in 2025, with workers leading over half of them by the final quarter. In response the Chinese Cyberspace Administration has directed social media platforms to remove protest content, and China Labour Bulletin, which kept the only public strike map, has been shut down after an investigation.

Thirty to 35% of all capitalists are CCP members, and many of the big capitalists, the 'princelings', are literally children of high-up CCP leaders. CCP propaganda has an effect, so too does education and upbringing. Ultimately, their own material class interests will be decisive in the outlook of the Chinese capitalists. When Stalinism in Russia collapsed, no amount of schooling in Stalinism prevented the oligarchs from stealing as much of the state's resources as they could.

The primary reason that the majority of the capitalist class accepts CCP constraints is because, up to now, the rapid growth of the Chinese economy has made it worth their while. And the most important factor is their fear of the working class entering the scene of history. Those factors are not going to contain tensions at the top indefinitely, although they may initially appear in a partially disguised way.

In future, tensions are not likely to be expressed as just the CCP on one side and pro-Western capitalists on the other. Much more likely are splits within the CCP. Whatever the initial basis for conflict, class struggle is going to ramp up in China over the coming period.

In this situation, there is no prospect of China acting in the developing world economic crisis as it did in the Great Recession, as a prop for the world economy, while the US was the banker of last resort. On the contrary, increased inter-imperialist rivalry, instability and conflict are on the rise and are acting to massively increase economic instability and crises.

Why the inflationary crisis came when it did

While there is debate among different sections of the capitalists about the reasons for, and the likely longevity of, the current bout of inflation, most broadly agree on the immediate factors that triggered it developing in the way, and at the time, it did.

One was the consequences of the pandemic stimulus packages. In the US in 2020, for example, the Federal Reserve pumped more than $3 trillion into the economy. One fifth of all the dollars in existence, physically and electronically, were created that year. But it was not just the US. The ratio of 'broad money' to GDP rose by almost as much in the Eurozone, by more in Japan, and most of all in Britain. Only now, after two years of quantitative tightening, and the pain of an inflation surge and a brutal cost-of-living squeeze for the working and middle classes, are ratios back to broadly pre-pandemic levels. However, central banks are having to tighten with extreme caution.

Not only was this an unprecedentedly large stimulus package, it was of a different character to the many rounds of quantitative easing, which in the main went to the super rich. The Bank of England, for example, estimated in 2012 that its QE up until then had ended in the hands of only the top 5% of the population. The UK government's furlough scheme during the pandemic, by contrast, paid 80% of the wages of over ten million workers, which meant they accrued some savings and were more likely to spend their money when the economy reopened.

The eruption of the Ukraine war, in addition to the untold human misery it has unleashed, has of course also massively exacerbated the supply chain problems, particularly when it comes to energy and food. And then, as even capitalist institutions like the IMF have had to acknowledge, major corporations inevitably took advantage of the situation to further increase their profits by hiking up prices.

In their response to the crisis the central banks are walking a tightrope, with little room for manoeuvre. The destabilising consequences of the surge in inflation mean they are compelled to try and drive it down by raising interest rates.

Yet as a consequence of this we saw the second and third biggest bank collapses in US history, followed by the failure of the Swiss banking behemoth, Credit Suisse. A new worldwide financial crisis seemed to loom, and with it another Great Recession akin to 2007-08. No more banks have failed since, but that does not tell us much.

Previous assertions that stronger regulation of the banks post-2008 would prevent future financial crises are in tatters. In fact the stress tests demanded by the Basel Regulatory Framework for banking do not even account for the possible consequences of higher interest rates. They also only require banks to be able to cope with maximum losses of 10% a day, whereas during the run on Silicon Valley Bank digital banking meant depositors removed almost a quarter of the bank's assets in just a few hours, while First Republic lost an incredible 90% of its uninsured deposits.

Nor is it only banks which are at risk. During the fiasco of the Liz Truss premiership in Britain it was pension funds that were in danger, while US insurance companies are also on the danger list, holding about $2.25 trillion of assets deemed to be risky, almost double the level they held in 2008, and representing about a third of their assets. There are multiple possibilities for the inevitable next financial shock to hit the global economy.

Although many capitalists are congratulating themselves on a soft landing from the period of very high inflation, there is no period of stability ahead.

The trading accounts of US banks topped $1 trillion in the third quarter of this year. Their trading accounts only reached this level once before, in the first quarter of 2008, just before the crash. That growth has left the banks, particularly the largest ones, more exposed to market moves than at any time since the financial crisis as they hold ever-greater inventories of price-sensitive securities.

Before Trump was even elected the banks had forced the Fed into a complete capitulation on the Basel 3 reforms, most noticeably on the amount of capital required to be held by the banks, which was halved from 19% to 9%. Trump is rumoured to be considering pulling out of the deal altogether.

The rise of protectionism

Biden successfully got three major Acts signed into law: the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, the CHIPS Act, and the largest, the Inflation Reduction Act, which focuses on a transition to clean energy. The total estimated cost was over $1.5 trillion. The reality of the acts has fallen far short of what was hoped for. Nonetheless, US manufacturing employment is reported to have increased by 700,000 between 2021 and March 2024. Factory building has contributed almost one third of business investment growth since the pandemic. Foreign direct investment into the US has increased from $4 trillion in 2017 to $5.3 trillion in 2022. In particular, the world's third and fourth economic powers, Germany and Japan, have increased their investment into the US by more than 50% since 2017.

The rest of the world is being forced to follow suit. The IMF estimates that in 2023 there were 2,500 new industrial policies introduced worldwide, 71% of which it considered to be trade distorting. This compares to virtually none a decade before.

All of these are indications of the increasingly multipolar character of the world, and the growing tensions between the major economic blocs. Nonetheless, the huge integration that took place in the world economy over recent decades has not been undone. The world economy still remains highly integrated. In the period after the collapse of Stalinism up until the 2008 Great Recession world trade grew dramatically from 39% as a percentage of global GDP in 1990 to 61% by 2008, with two-thirds of the profits growth to 2013 being captured by Western companies. Since 2008 world trade has not regained that peak but has still remained at a higher level than the early 2000s, or any previous period of history.

This year World Trade Organisation economists divided the world into two geopolitical blocs centred on the US and China and looked at the difference between trade within the blocs and between them since the invasion of Ukraine. They found that goods trade between the blocs had grown by only 4.2% less than it had within them.

In reality, this indicates that there are not two defined blocs, but rather different national capitalist classes attempting to defend their own interests, often balancing between the big players. Nonetheless, the increasingly multipolar and conflict-ridden character of world capitalism is going to make it far harder to achieve effective global cooperation to limit the consequences of the next major economic crisis whenever it comes.

Even for the US, still the richest country, any positive consequences of protectionist measures are likely to be very limited, especially for the working class. Given the high level of automation at the manufacturing plants being built, new jobs being created are likely to be relatively small scale. At the same time increased tariffs raise the price of goods for the US working class, at a time when the global inflation surge has already led to a 20% increase in prices since 2021.

Biden's Inflation Reduction Act is above all part of the trade war against China, and it attempted to draw Western allies fully behind it. However, different economic pressures limit the degree of agreement on the approach to China. According to the Economist, Germany is twice as reliant on China's economy as the US.

The IMF writes that "a fragmented world is unlikely to achieve progress for all or to allow us to tackle global challenges such as climate change or pandemic preparedness". Their plea to the capitalists is that "we must avoid that path at all costs", but that is clearly the path to disasters that the system is on.

Being able to pay its debts in its own currency has been an enormous advantage for US imperialism. But the price is the huge accumulation of debt with the rest of the world. This is arguably the biggest time bomb underlying the global economy. At a certain point, this debt will become absolutely unsustainable, with a collapse of the US bond market and the value of the dollar. Capitalist leaders internationally are well aware of this problem, but are completely unable to steer an orderly transition to an alternative system of reserve currencies, either through a shared system based on major currencies such as the euro, yen and yuan, or on special drawing rights administered by the IMF. A collapse of the dollar would mean global currency chaos and could itself provoke a new, even deeper downturn in the world economy.

Conclusion

Underlying the deficit and surplus relationship between countries are even deeper economic and social contradictions. There is a deepening chasm, globally and within rich and poor countries alike, between the super-rich capitalists and the mass of the population, the workers, labourers and small farmers who toil to produce the wealth. This extreme class polarisation poses the threat of social and political upheavals, a threat recently recognised by the more far-sighted capitalist strategists. At the same time, the falling share of wages in national income and the erosion of mass living standards is further restricting the market for capitalism.

There is not a simple, unvarying relationship between wage levels and the market for capitalist goods. Nevertheless, in the last analysis, the capitalists have to sell the goods and services they produce in order to realise surplus value in the form of profit. Even the sober Economist warned about the future of capitalism becoming "wealth without workers, workers without wealth".

Inequality began to widen more than two decades ago in the US. However, general growth and an ability to borrow helped to cover this up for considerable sections of the population. During Ronald Reagan's first six years in office, 1981 to 1986, GDP grew by 22% while median income grew 6%. During Bill Clinton's first six years, 1993 to 1998, GDP grew by 24%, median income 11%. Growth began to slow from 2000, undermining both the mean and median figures. In George Bush's first six years, GDP rose by 16%, but median incomes fell by 2%. Under Barack Obama it was even worse: GDP up 8%, and median income down 4%, according to the Census Bureau.

Global inequality is even more pronounced. As the Economist writes: "In 1820 the world's richest country, Britain, was about five times richer than the average poor nation. Now America is about 25 times wealthier than the average poor country".

Despite the favourable conditions for capitalism since the collapse of the Stalinist states, the system has not overcome the problems of over-accumulation. In fact, it has become even more acute. The rapid growth of China's economy, moreover, has not reversed this tendency. Over-accumulation is not just a short-term, conjunctural problem. It expresses an organic crisis of the system. Capital, as Marx predicted, has reached an impasse within the framework of private ownership of the means of production and the nation state, despite the increased integration of the global market. Only planning, under the democratic control of the working class, will make it possible to develop production to a higher stage, satisfying social needs rather than the greed for profit, and to organise production on a global basis.
