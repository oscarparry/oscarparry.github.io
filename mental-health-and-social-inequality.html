Mental health and social inequality

4 May 2019 HealthInequality

Written as an undergraduate. Biological and psychological factors shape who becomes ill, but the evidence that social adversity drives mental health outcomes across whole populations is hard to argue with.

Set question: To what extent are mental health problems determined by social inequality?

Mental health problems are a global issue; according to the World Health Organization's 2014 survey, depression is estimated to affect around 300 million people worldwide, 4.4% of the world's population [1]. A better understanding of the causes of mental health problems can reduce the stigma surrounding mental health and inform people of how best to help friends and family who are suffering. Increased knowledge can also inform government policy, ensuring money spent on mental health services goes to projects that are as effective as possible. The 2014 Adult Psychiatric Morbidity Survey reported that 17% of adults surveyed in England met the criteria for at least one common mental health disorder [2]. Mental health costs the UK an estimated £70-100bn through lost productivity, social benefits and health care, equivalent to 4.5% of GDP [3]. These figures show mental health problems affect a huge proportion of our society; a better understanding of their causes will have significant benefits.

An understanding of what causes mental health problems has evolved over time. The spread of Christianity in the middle ages led to a belief that mental health problems were caused by demonic forces, which needed to be exorcised by a priest. The opening of Bethlehem day hospital in 1247 at Charing Cross, London, marked a move towards a more institutionalised system of mental health care [4]. The accidental discovery of psychotropic drugs such as lithium in 1949, as well as the later use of benzodiazepines to help depression and neuroleptics to reduce the symptoms of schizophrenia, cemented the belief that there is a biological cause of mental health problems. An early proponent of the biological model was Emil Kraepelin, who was instrumental in developing a classification system for mental health disorders. This later led to the development of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, which was made with the aim of helping clinicians diagnose the same symptoms with the same clinical label. Eric Kandel is a modern advocate of the biological model; his influential paper 'A New Intellectual Framework for Psychiatry' proposes that all human behaviour is determined by brain functioning, arguing that we should take a neuroscientific approach to solving mental health problems [5]. However, a biological explanation of mental health problems has come under attack by Professor Shôn Lewis, who thinks it implies irreversibility. Lewis thinks that the biological model may lead people to assume that mental health issues cannot be helped, placing the issue within the person suffering. There have been problems with the reliability and validity of classification systems, leading to ongoing criticism of their use, especially by Robert Whitaker, who argues that a diagnosis does not make any reference to precipitating life events [6]. Steven S. Sharfstein also warns against the profitability of antipsychotic medication, with antidepressant medication ranking third in pharmaceutical sales worldwide [7]. This could lead to pharmaceutical companies pushing the biological model on consumers to increase profits.

Several other non-biological factors contribute to a person's mental health; much research has been done on the link between economic inequality and the risk of developing a mental health disorder. Analysis of 115 studies showed that 70% reported positive associations between a variety of poverty measures and common mental disorders [1]. In the UK alone, 14 million people live in poverty, over one in five of the population. Rising income inequality, poverty, reduced social mobility, and the growing number of people living in insecure, overcrowded and unsafe housing are all social issues associated with poorer mental health [1]. Depressed mood or anxiety was found to be 2.5 times higher among young people aged 10-15 with low socioeconomic status than among youths with high economic status. Since 50% of mental health problems are established by the age of 15, this is clear evidence that lower socioeconomic position can lead to worse mental health. Mental health problems can lead to lower lifelong earnings, leading to a cross-generational perpetuation of inequalities, known as the social selection hypothesis [8]. However not everyone who has a low socioeconomic status develops mental health problems, so other factors must contribute.

How we think about ourselves and our lives contributes to our mental health. The tendency to blame oneself for negative events is associated with low mood and depression. Rumination is another factor contributing to low moods [9]. Ruminators are more likely to judge their problems as more difficult, and more likely to believe they may be unable to solve them, indicating that the way we think impacts our mental health [10]. According to the American Psychological Association, resilience to problematic events can be learned and taught [11]. 70% of people with high resilience, compared to 41% of people with low resilience, report high well-being. This data shows that a person's psychological state can protect them against mental health problems.

Racism and discrimination are also important risk factors for developing mental health issues. Kimberlé Crenshaw developed intersectionality theory to explain how multiple sources of inequality and oppression are interconnected within social group categorisations such as race, social class, gender and disability [12]. Black, Asian and minority ethnic groups in the UK are more likely to be diagnosed with mental health problems than their White British peers. Women are almost twice as likely to be diagnosed with depression and anxiety as men [13]. However, some argue that this could be due to under-diagnosis among men. Men were three times more likely than women to take their own lives in the UK in 2018. Erving Goffman defines stigma as a negative characteristic that segregates an individual from society [14]. Social identity theory states that categorisation and stigmatisation result in an 'in group' and an 'out group' in society. People experiencing mental health problems are more likely to be perceived by themselves and others as belonging to a minority 'out group' [15]. Corker found that nine out of ten individuals with mental health difficulties reported having experienced stigma related to their diagnoses [16]. This can lead to reduced social support networks, an enhanced issue for migrants. Lack of access to formal healthcare can worsen mental health issues; NHS figures indicate that white British people experiencing mental health difficulties are twice as likely to access formal care as someone from a Black, Asian or minority ethnic community [17]. Those service users report discrimination, stigma, and more coercive care, deterring many from seeking formal help. These examples indicate how social identity and social roles interrelate to reinforce social and structural inequalities.

James Nazroo has researched the link between socioeconomic position and social isolation after retirement, finding a clear correlation. Those most likely to remain socially engaged in later life are those in a more affluent socioeconomic position. In the UK the chance for those in the richest quintile to move into social detachment is approximately 20% of the chance of those in the poorest quintile [18]. Coupled with the fact that individuals living in households with the lowest 20% income are two to three times more likely to develop mental health problems than those in the highest 20% [1], this is fairly conclusive evidence that socioeconomic position contributes to mental health. Neighbourhood factors such as noise levels, crime, traffic and air and water quality are all linked to poorer mental health. Living in socially advantaged neighbourhoods with interpersonal trust, proximity and access to nature and better access to healthcare are associated with better mental health and the ability to recover from mental health problems.

There are also high levels of inequality in access to formal mental health treatment in many societies. In all countries there is a significant gap between the number of people in need of mental health interventions and those who receive it, known as the treatment gap. In low and middle income countries, the treatment gap ranges from 76.3% to 85.4% [1], and can even reach 100% for individuals with severe mental illnesses [19]. In high income countries, the treatment gap ranges from 35% to 50% [1]. 45% of the world's population lives in a country where there is less than one psychiatrist to serve 100,000 people, so many people's mental health problems go untreated [1].

However lack of access to formal healthcare does not necessarily mean worse rates of mental health. Many people receive help and support from within social networks, turning to family, friends and spiritual sources. An example of this is the Zimbabwean Friendship Bench project, a stepped care approach to depression. A controlled trial by the University of Zimbabwe and King's College London found that whereas 50% of people who received standard care still had depression, this figure was 14% among those who used the Friendship Bench [20].

In conclusion, although there are biological and psychological characteristics that increase people's vulnerability or resilience to the onset of mental health difficulties, there is compelling evidence for the existence of social risk factors. The chronic stress of social adversity can lead to lifelong mental and physical health problems, so reducing social inequality will have a positive effect on the mental health of those facing social adversity. Increasing access to mental healthcare and using culturally informed interventions would be an effective way to reduce mental health problems worldwide. There is now considerable scientific consensus that reducing child poverty and giving every child the best possible start in life will generate the greatest societal and mental health benefits.
