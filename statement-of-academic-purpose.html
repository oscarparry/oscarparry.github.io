Statement of academic purpose

27 April 2022 InequalityReading

What I had been reading, and why I wanted to study inequality formally. Written as a physics graduate applying to a social science department, which meant making the case for the reading rather than the credentials.

First published in written for the LSE MSc application, 2022

The interdisciplinary approach offered by the MSc in Inequalities and Social Science is what makes the course particularly appealing to me. The opportunity to be taught by academics from a range of departments makes this MSc stand out. I view this MSc as an essential part of my future plans. I hope to go on to apply for a PhD studying inequalities, so both the qualitative and quantitative analysis modules of the programme would be indispensable in transitioning to using statistics in a social science setting and learning to design my own research projects. The indicative reading for all the modules on the programme looks fascinating; modules that were of specific interest to me include the Anthropology of the Economy and Taxation of Wealth modules. Although I do not have an academic background in the social sciences, my degree in physics has equipped me with many transferable skills. These include familiarity with analytical methods, empirical reasoning, problem-solving abilities, as well as a strong grasp of maths and statistics. Modules in my degree that had a sizeable statistical element included Random Processes in Physics and Statistical Mechanics.

The current research themes at the International Inequalities Institute are exactly the kind of work I want to be involved with in the future. I found the working paper 'The UK's Global Economic Elite' particularly relevant due to the recent revelations of Akshata Murthy's non-dom status. The paper exposes the link between non-dom status and earnings, finding that less than 0.1% of those earning less than £100k annually claim non-dom status, rising to 30% of those earning over £5 million. I was also impressed by the paper 'National Responsibility for Ecological Breakdown: A Fair-Shares Assessment of Resource Use, 1970-2017.' The paper finds that high-income countries, with only 16% of the world's population, are responsible for 74% of cumulative excess resource use worldwide. This is another incredibly relevant topic, considering the recent release of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change's findings that greenhouse emissions must be cut in half by 2030 to limit global warming to 1.5C.

I have read widely in the social sciences while studying for my undergraduate degree. David Graeber's books have had a large impact on me. In The Dawn of Everything I found the idea of 'the indigenous critique of Europe' by Kandiaronk particularly fascinating. Debt: The First 5,000 Years changed my conceptions of how economics and morality evolve in different societies, revealing the importance of moral framing in supporting the overwhelming debt of the global south. Other books on economic history that impacted me included Thomas Piketty's Capital, which challenges many perceived wisdoms in economics. His work disputes the Kuznets curve, arguing that it is a post-war aberration in the relentless accumulation of wealth by the top echelons of society. How Europe Underdeveloped Africa by Walter Rodney drove home the massive impact of the transatlantic slave trade on the 'underdevelopment' of Africa, arguing that the idea of naturally 'underdeveloped' countries is a colonial myth. Noam Chomsky's Who Rules the World and How the World Works introduced me to the critique of global institutions like the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund, as well as asserting that free trade agreements have a large role in producing international inequalities.

The Divide by Jason Hickel offered comprehensive coverage of the historical roots of inequality, so I was delighted to find out he is associated with the Inequalities Institute. Reading his book I learnt of the International Centre for the Settlement of Investment Disputes, which allows corporations to sue governments for impacting their 'expected future profits' if they try to pass legislation to protect the environment, as in the infamous 1997 Metalclad v Mexico case. Hickel exposed me to the major flaws in the World Bank's poverty measurements. He also introduced me to organisations such as Global Financial Integrity and the Institute for Fiscal Studies, which work towards changing economic discourse and the 'good news narrative' peddled by mainstream media. A key takeaway from this book was the role of tax havens in contributing to global inequality. Since then I have looked into major tax haven data leaks published by the International Consortium of Investigative Journalists, including reading the entertaining book The Panama Papers by Bastian Obermayer and Frederik Obermaier.

Distinction by Pierre Bourdieu introduced me to the concept of cultural inequality through class stratification, how the idea of 'good taste' being a natural talent that one is born with is used by the upper classes to assert their superiority over the working class. I was happy to see a book in the same vein written by a lecturer at LSE: Comedy and Distinction by Sam Friedman. I am also a big fan of LSE Professor Mike Savage, especially his work on the Great British Class Survey and his book Social Class in the 21st Century. His idea that 'increasing mobility within the education system will enhance, not reduce class inequality since those able to draw on the greatest resources will do better' I found echoed in another thought-provoking book, The Tyranny of Merit by Michael Sandel. This is a particularly personal topic for me, having worked in the education system for the last four years. I have been working as a maths, science and English tutor for a Manchester-based charity, teaching students from a variety of backgrounds, including students with special educational needs who have not been in a traditional school setting for a number of years. Drawing on this experience, I completely agree that focusing specifically on success in the education system is a false idol for reducing inequalities. A broader approach must be taken to give everyone the resources they need to live full lives.

I believe it is essential to study inequalities from an intersectional standpoint. Reading Kimberlé Crenshaw's 'Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex', Angela Davis' Women, Race and Class and Judith Butler's Gender Trouble was an important milestone for me. Viewing inequality between societal groups as not just based on race, gender, class, or sexuality, but best understood as being influenced by all these factors at once, is key to a greater understanding of the causes of inequality. Edward Said's Orientalism exposed me to the long-lasting effects of colonialism on shaping establishment views of 'the Other.' His ideas of cultural domination and the functioning of power are still incredibly relevant in our increasingly globalised society. His comparison of Arthur James Balfour and Lord Cromer describing the British role in Egypt with Henry Kissinger's essay 'Domestic Structure and Foreign Policy' reveals that the underlying concepts of Western superiority over 'the Orient' have been reproduced in the current discourse about 'developed' and 'developing' countries. Michel Foucault's Discipline and Punish introduced me to many thought-provoking ideas about how power and knowledge are used to assert class domination through the creation of 'delinquency,' as well as how much the role and types of punishment have changed over the past few hundred years, culminating in the prison-industrial complex and surveillance state we have today. The Age of Surveillance Capitalism by Shoshana Zuboff revealed the size of the 'revolving door' between US government legislators creating data protection laws and firms like Google and Facebook who actively acquire data.

To borrow a phrase I first encountered in Owen Jones' book The Establishment, I believe it is of paramount importance to change the 'Overton window' with regards to what can be done to reduce inequalities in the world today. As a society, we vitally need to shift our ideas of what is both politically possible and necessary, in order to avert an imminent climate disaster that will disproportionately affect the global South and to allow billions of people worldwide who currently do not have access to enough food, water, medicine or education to live a dignified life. I believe the International Inequalities Institute contributes massively to this task. I would relish the opportunity to study under some of the people who have inspired me to apply for an MSc in this subject.
