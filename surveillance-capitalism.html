Surveillance capitalism and the logic of accumulation

20 March 2020 TechnologyEconomics

Written as an undergraduate. The usual complaint about Google and Facebook is that individual privacy is eroded. That is misleading. What actually happens is that the right to decide about privacy is taken away from people and concentrated in a few firms.

First published in interdisciplinary course essay, University of Manchester

The cyber-enabled harmful activity that I have chosen to focus on is the invasion of privacy by corporations such as Google or Facebook. These companies render individuals' activities online as 'behavioural data' which they then use to precisely target advertisements to individuals who are viewed as most susceptible to the ad. In this way the companies are seeking not just to gather as much of our behavioural data as possible, but to change the very nature of our behaviour, in order to increase their advertising revenues. For companies to target advertisements more effectively than the competition, they must be able to obtain more data. This leads to a 'logic of accumulation' whereby companies will use every resource at their disposal to obtain more data. The pursuit of behavioural data for profit is known as 'surveillance capitalism' [1], and is leading to large technological change even in the offline world, but for whose benefit is this change occurring?

The reason that surveillance capitalism has come to prominence is the hyper-connectivity of devices we use in our everyday lives. Your computer and smartphone are constantly gathering huge amounts of data; your location, your emails and even what you say can be 'datafied' and sold to third parties in order to create a data set known as 'User Profile Information.' A 2015 analysis of the most visited one million websites found that 90% send user data to an average of nine external domains. 79% of these sites send data to a domain owned by Google, while 34% send data to a domain owned by Facebook [2]. The scale of raw data that is available is reflected in Google's domination of the internet search market. Google processes over 40,000 search queries every second on average; more than 3.5 billion searches every day and 1.2 trillion searches per year worldwide [3]. Hyperconnectivity also means that corporations can get instant feedback on what advertisements are most effective on any individual. Companies can use machine learning algorithms to test a variety of different ads, honing their product until it is most effective at altering the behaviour of the individual. The importance of advertising for online companies cannot be understated: in 2016, 89 per cent of the revenue of Alphabet, Google's parent company, came from advertising programs [4].

The 'logic of accumulation' that prevails among firms that use behavioural data means they must be constantly finding new ways to obtain data in order to stay ahead of the competition. This has led to firms pushing a new agenda onto society, known as the Internet of Things. Gartner, a private technology research agency, defines the Internet of Things as "a network of physical objects that contain embedded technology to communicate and sense or interact with their internal states or the external environment" [5]. This is a logical progression for surveillance capitalism; the devices on the Internet of Things, known as smart devices, are embedded with sensors and software, a treasure trove of behavioural data. The cycle of dispossession of privacy rights can be illustrated by the example of Google Street View. This service takes a picture of every public space, claiming them for Google in the quest for more data. However the main invasion of privacy here was discovered by the German Federal Commission for Data Protection. In 2010 they announced that Google's Street View cars were collecting personal data from unencrypted private Wi-Fi networks without permission [6]. The personal data was found to include names, credit information, passwords, messages, medical information, pornography, browsing data, photos and videos, a huge breach of privacy [7].

The typical complaint against surveillance capitalism is that the privacy of the individual is being eroded, but that is misleading. In the larger societal pattern, decision rights over privacy are redistributed, claimed by a select few companies. Instead of people having the rights to decide how and what they will disclose, these rights are concentrated within the domain of surveillance capitalism. US Supreme Court Justice William O. Douglas articulated this view of privacy in 1967: "Privacy involves the choice of the individual to disclose or to reveal what he believes, what he thinks, what he possesses" [8]. As a global community there must be new social and legal conventions in order for people to retain their dignity, privacy, and autonomy. However, firms disguise their activities under a quasi-legal guise, in the form of click-through terms and conditions that purport to protect a user's privacy, but in fact give the firm permission to pass on data to a host of third parties. Firms also make these privacy policies unnecessarily long and tortuous so it is impossible for the average internet user to read them. In 2008 two Carnegie Mellon professors calculated that reading all the privacy policies that an individual encounters in a year would require 76 workdays, a cost to the US economy of $781 billion [9]. Firms also invest heavily in reducing government oversight of their activities. In 2017, Google spent $18 million on lobbying in the United States, more than any other corporation [10]. The Google Transparency Project investigated the movement of staff between the Google sphere (the company and its lobbying firms) and the American government. It found that by April 2016, 61 members of staff had moved from the Google sphere into the government, and 197 had moved the other way [11].

To effectively combat the invasion of privacy, the government must legislate to protect the individual from the rampant seizure of data by firms. The EU's General Data Protection Regulation, implemented in 2018, requires firms to anonymise collected data and obtain the consent of individuals to gather data about them [12]. However in practice it is almost impossible for an individual to opt out of data gathering, as many online services lose functionality if their terms and services are not agreed to. Firms are essentially holding their services to ransom in exchange for an individual's data. Although the GDPR is a step in the right direction, I believe that many more regulations must be put in place to protect the privacy of the individual in our hyperconnected society, or else we risk an existential threat to the autonomy of the individual.
