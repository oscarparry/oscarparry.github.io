The 1926 general strike: working-class power on display

24 June 2026 BritainHistoryTrade unionsMarxism

A leadoff written for the centenary of the general strike. For nine days the country stopped, the strike was solid and growing, and that is the moment its own leaders surrendered. Why, and what follows from it.

Written as a thirty-minute leadoff for a Socialist Party public meeting marking the centenary of the general strike, with roughly fifteen minutes of reply notes after it. The timing cues have been taken out; the reply notes are kept at the end, because they are where most of the arguments actually got had. Light copy-editing only.

The country stops

Today we are commemorating the great general strike of 1926. One hundred years ago, for nine days, this country stopped.

The trains didn't run. The buses didn't run. The docks were silent. The newspapers didn't print. Three and a half million workers had downed tools at the same moment, on the instruction of the Trades Union Congress, and behind those three and a half million stood their families, their streets, their communities. The whole of working-class Britain, moving as one.

In the second week of the strike, King George the Fifth was talking to one of the trade union leaders, a railwaymen's leader called Jimmy Thomas. And the King, who was, by the accounts, badly shaken, gestured around him at the palace, at all of it, and said: "Well, Thomas, if the worst happens, I suppose all this will vanish?"

All this will vanish. That is the question a general strike puts on the table. Not "can we get a few pence on the wage." The question is: who actually runs society? Who keeps it moving, and who is just sitting on top of it, creaming off the profits?

That is what we are here to talk about tonight. Because 1926 is the closest that Britain has come to overthrowing this rotten system of capitalism, where the labour of the majority goes to the profits of a tiny minority. And the opportunity was thrown away, not by the workers, but by the people at the top of their own movement. Understanding how and why is not an abstract part of history; it's a lesson we can learn from to ensure that the same mistakes are never repeated.

What is a general strike?

Let me start with the basics. What actually is a general strike?

An ordinary strike is one group of workers, in one workplace, or one industry, withdrawing their labour to win a demand. Better pay, safer conditions, stopping a sacking. It's the most basic weapon workers have: the bosses need us to work, so when we stop, we have leverage. Most of you will have been on strike, or know someone who has.

A general strike is something different in kind, not just in size. It's when workers across the whole economy strike together, not just bin workers, or just railworkers, but everyone, all at once. And the moment that happens, something changes. Because if nothing moves without the working class, then the working class is suddenly the only force in society that can make things move at all. Food, fuel, transport, power, all of it now depends on permission from the workers.

So a general strike, whatever it's called over, immediately raises a far bigger question than the one it started with. It raises the question of power.

The British ruling class understood this perfectly well, better, frankly, than some of the trade union leaders did. The Liberal prime minister Lloyd George sat the union leaders down and said this to them: if you strike, you will beat us, but have you thought about what comes next? A strike against the government, by its very success, becomes a challenge to the state itself. And then, he said, "if a force arises that is stronger than the state itself, they must be ready to take on the functions of the state, or withdraw and accept the authority of the state." "Gentlemen, have you considered, and if you have, are you ready?"

One of those union leaders said afterwards: "from that moment on, we were beaten." Because Lloyd George had taken their measure. He'd worked out that they would never, ever, be "ready" to take power, and that all he had to do, when the time came, was force them to confront that choice. This is the tragedy of 1926.

Why it happened: the roots

So why did it happen in 1926? General strikes don't fall from the sky, and they can't be conjured up by a few activists making a speech. They come out of a head-on collision between the two great classes in society, and that collision was being prepared for years.

Here's the background. Britain had just come out of the First World War as, on paper, a victor. But underneath, British capitalism was in deep trouble. For a century it had been the "workshop of the world", it had got rich first, dominated world trade, run the largest empire in history. But by the 1920s it was being overtaken, by America, by Germany, and its old industries were clapped out and under-invested. Coal, cotton, steel, the railways: decrepit, inefficient, falling behind.

Now, everyone agreed these industries needed modernising. The question, and this is the question in every economic crisis, including ours, was: modernised how, and in whose interests? Who pays?

The bosses had one answer, and one answer only: make the workers pay. Cut wages, lengthen hours, drive down the share of the wealth going to labour so as to restore profits. But they had to be careful with how they went about it. "The whole of Europe is in a revolutionary mood", the British prime minister David Lloyd George wrote to the Paris Peace Conference, meeting after the end of hostilities in the first world war. "The whole of the existing social, political and economic order is being called into question by the mass of the people". Britain was no exception. Faced with revolts in India and Ireland, the British ruling class was also confronted by a radicalised working class at home. In 1919 there was an enormous upsurge of strikes, 35 million days lost, as workers struggled for better conditions and to strengthen their organisations. In many areas, councils of action, soviets in all but name, sprang up to prevent British intervention against the October 1917 revolution in Russia.

And it came to a head in the coalmines. Coal was the heart of the economy then, it powered everything, and it employed three-quarters of a million miners, in one of the strongest unions in the country, the Miners' Federation. The mines were privately owned, by some of the most reactionary employers in Britain, and the only "reorganisation" they were interested in was longer hours and lower pay.

There were a series of warning tremors before the earthquake. In 1921 there was a day the movement never forgot, called Black Friday, when the leaders of the other big unions, the so-called triple alliance of railwaymen's, transport workers' and miners' unions, broke a promise to strike alongside the miners and left them to fight alone. The miners were beaten. Wages were cut across industry. The working class was thrown onto the back foot.

Then in 1925 two things happened that lit the fuse. The Conservative government put Britain back on the gold standard, and you don't need the economics, just the effect: it made British coal more expensive to sell abroad, which meant the coal-owners immediately demanded yet another round of wage cuts to compensate. And the owners came forward with their terms: a thirteen per cent pay cut, and an extra hour added to the working day. The miners' answer became one of the great slogans of the British labour movement: "Not a penny off the pay, not a minute on the day."

In July 1925 the government wasn't ready for the fight, so it blinked. It paid a temporary subsidy to keep wages up, and set up a commission to look into the industry. The trade union right wing crowed that this was a great victory, Red Friday, they called it. But the more clear-sighted people in the movement weren't fooled. The miners' president, Herbert Smith, told his members, plainly: this is not a victory, "it is only an armistice." The government was simply buying time.

And what did they do with that time? They prepared for war. They stockpiled. They appointed Civil Commissioners to run every region in an emergency. They set up a strike-breaking organisation to keep supplies moving. They got ready, methodically, to take on and defeat the entire labour movement.

And the labour leaders? On the workers' side? They did nothing. No plan. No preparation. No strategy. Because, as we'll see, in their hearts they didn't want the fight at all.

The commission reported in March 1926. Its proposal: reorganise the industry, and cut wages. The owners locked the miners out. And on the first of May 1926, the TUC was forced to act. Its delegates voted for a general strike in support of the miners, and look at the size of that vote: three and a half million in favour, against fifty thousand. It was overwhelming. Two days later, the country stopped.

The nine days: workers taste power

Now I want to spend real time here, because this is the part of the story that gets buried, and it's the most important part. What actually happened during those nine days? Millions of ordinary people discovered, almost overnight, that they were capable of running society themselves.

The first thing was the sheer scale of the response. The leaders had made no preparations, but the rank and file didn't need them to. The TUC's own report, dazed, on the fourth of May: the response has "surpassed all our expectations… the difficulty has been to keep men in the second line of defence rather than call them out." Workers who weren't even asked to strike were striking. The solidarity was total.

But here's the thing that makes 1926 extraordinary. When you stop the whole country, you create a problem, even for the strikers. Food still has to move. Hospitals still need supplies. So who decides what moves and what doesn't? And the answer, across the country, was: the workers decided.

In town after town, the local trades councils, these were just the ordinary local committees of the unions, transformed themselves into something new. They called them Councils of Action. And they were, in everything but name, embryos of a workers' government. Around four hundred trades councils and over a hundred Councils of Action sprang up. They set up sub-committees for everything a society needs: transport, food distribution, communications by motorcycle courier, printing their own bulletins, organising the pickets, even organising a workers' defence corps.

Let me give you one example. In the North East, Northumberland and Durham, the Council of Action drew up what it called a "Plan of Campaign." And the plan stated its objective in black and white: to defeat the Civil Commissioner, the government's man, the official sent to break the strike with emergency powers, and to build, across the whole region, "a network of Councils of Action linked up with a central directing body whose authority and scope would exactly answer to the Civil Commissioner." In other words: we will build, right now, a rival authority to the government's, and we will out-organise it.

And they did. Within forty hours, the government's Civil Commissioner, the representative of the British state, came, at night, to negotiate, in person, with the strike committee. Later he came back, with the regional army commander beside him, to plead with the workers for permission to move food under "dual control." The strike committee said no. "We cannot agree to our men working under any form of dual control." And they withdrew the permits.

Think about what that means. The British army was asking a committee of trade unionists for permission to move lorries. Across the country, lorries that were allowed to move had to carry a placard saying, "By permission of the TUC." The levers of power were physically passing into the hands of the working class, and everyone could feel it.

The workers could feel it most of all. In Methil, in Fife, at the end of the strike, one of the organisers found the men around him thinking out loud: "Well, in another day or two we'd better walk into power." In a working-class district in London, the Labour Party reported that after three days, if a speaker mentioned the coal-owners the crowd was bored, but the moment you mentioned the government, the crowd was electric. Ordinary people had grasped, by instinct, that this was no longer about coal. This was about the government and the TUC. About them and us. About who runs the country.

The Russian revolutionary Leon Trotsky, who watched all this closely, and whose warnings run right through this story, said that if the British working class had had a leadership that matched its strength, power could have passed into its hands within weeks. He wasn't exaggerating for effect. Even the ruling class believed it. Churchill and Baldwin openly spoke of an "alternative government." The strike committees, as one honest historian admitted, were "clearly intending to substitute their own authority for that of the government," and given a few more weeks "may well have evolved into embryo soviets", workers' councils, like the ones that had run the Russian Revolution nine years earlier.

And the rest of society was not lined up solidly against the strikers. The middle class, the small business people, the professionals, were wavering, watching, far from sure the government was right. The government's volunteer strike-breakers were a joke, public schoolboys treating it as a lark, who couldn't actually keep the country running. The strike, in the words of the workers' own history of it, was "just beginning", its full force was only starting to be felt.

So let's be absolutely clear about the situation on day nine. The strike was solid. It was growing. The state's strike-breaking was failing. The other classes were wavering. By every measure, the working class was winning.

And that is the moment its own leaders surrendered.

The betrayal

Why would you call off a strike you are winning?

Because the right-wing leaders of the TUC had never wanted to win it. They had been dragged into it. And from the first hour, their one overriding aim was to end it, on almost any terms.

Jimmy Thomas, the railwaymen's leader, said it himself, before the strike even started. He said he dreaded the strike. And what he dreaded was not defeat, but victory getting out of control. His exact words: "What I dreaded about this strike more than anything was this, if by any chance it should have got out of the hands of those who would be able to exercise some control." Another leader said the strike was a disaster precisely because authority was "passing out of the hands of responsible executives into the hands of men who had no authority", meaning, into the hands of the workers themselves.

That is the heart of it. These leaders were more frightened of their own members than they were of the government. They were more frightened of victory than of defeat. Because a victorious general strike would have meant exactly what the King feared, that "all this will vanish", and the comfortable position of these gentlemen at the head of the trade union apparatus would vanish with it. They had spent their careers being flattered by the establishment, dining at the right tables; some of them, like Thomas, had served in government. They did not want to overthrow that world. They wanted a seat at the table.

So while millions stood firm, the leaders went looking for a way out. They opened secret negotiations, through the very man who'd chaired the coal commission, Herbert Samuel, who came back from his holiday in Italy to play the go-between. Samuel offered them a face-saving "formula", a compromise that, crucially, the government had not agreed to and was not bound by. It was worthless. But it was an excuse. And an excuse was all they were looking for.

On the twelfth of May, with the strike solid, with the government's own paper admitting "there is little sign of a general collapse", that very same day, the General Council called the whole thing off. Unconditionally. With no guarantees for anyone. The miners hadn't even agreed, and it was the miners they were supposed to be defending.

Even the Conservatives couldn't believe it. Baldwin reported to the King that his own MPs found it "difficult to believe that the surrender of the TUC was unconditional." They had handed over a winning position for nothing.

And the workers' reaction? Fury. At one mass meeting the news of the surrender was met with, in the record of the time, "furious boos and hissings." At another, workers threw the TUC's newspaper back in the faces of the people handing it out. One report read simply: "a victorious army disarmed and handed over to its enemies."

But here's the magnificent thing, the thing that tells you everything about the rank and file versus the leaders. The workers did not just go quietly back. When they saw the employers using the surrender to victimise activists and tear up agreements, more workers came out. On the tenth day, the day after the betrayal, there were a hundred thousand more workers on strike than on any day before. The leaders had thrown in the towel, and the army kept fighting anyway. It was that pressure, from below, that forced even the bosses and Baldwin to pull back from the worst of the victimisation.

The price, and the question of leadership

The miners were left to fight on alone, exactly as they had been on Black Friday five years before. They held out, in real hunger and hardship, until November, when they were starved back to work on the owners' terms: longer hours, lower pay, district-by-district.

And the following year, 1927, the government drove home the defeat in law. The Trades Disputes Act made general strikes and sympathy strikes illegal, crippled picketing, and cut the unions' funding to the Labour Party. The most reactionary anti-union law in over a century.

So we have to ask the hard question. The working class showed colossal strength. It built organs of its own power. It had society wavering its way. So why did it lose?

Not because it was too weak. That's the lie that's told. The strength was there. What was missing was at the top. What was missing was leadership, a leadership armed with a clear idea of where the strike had to go.

Now, there was a left wing on the General Council, and there was a revolutionary party in the form of the communist party. So why didn't they fill the gap?

The "left" leaders talked a magnificent game. They made fiery speeches about the coming "final struggle" against "wage slavery." But Trotsky had warned, before the strike, exactly what they were: a left that was, in his phrase, of "total ideological formlessness." All radical words, no worked-out programme, no plan to actually take power. And so, when the test came, they did precisely what he predicted. They wavered, and then they capitulated, and they retreated back behind the right wing. As Trotsky put it: the lefts "do not have a line of their own. They will go on swinging to the right under the pressure of [the establishment] and to the left under the pressure of the masses."

Which brings us to the part of the story I most want you to take home, because it is the bridge from 1926 to tonight, to us, to what we are doing in the Socialist Party.

The Minority Movement, and the Shop Stewards Network today

In the years before the strike, the most militant, class-conscious workers in Britain built up an organisation called the National Minority Movement.

It was launched in 1924, on the initiative of the young Communist Party. And it was a rank-and-file movement, a network that cut across all the different unions, bringing together the shop stewards, the branch activists, the militants, into one organised body to fight for fighting policies in every union at once. At its height, it spoke for a quarter of the entire trade union membership, well over a million workers. It had real authority, with leaders who were household names in the movement, like Tom Mann. It published papers, ran candidates for union positions, organised conferences of hundreds of delegates, backed every strike. By any measure, this was a powerful instrument.

And it still failed to change the outcome in May 1926. Why? This is the crucial lesson, and the Socialist Party has gone back over it carefully, because we don't want to repeat it.

The Minority Movement made a fatal error of method. Instead of putting concrete demands and warnings on the trade union leaders, instead of saying loudly, "these leaders are not preparing, here is what preparation would actually look like, and if the official leaders won't do it, we will", it increasingly just cheered them on. Its central slogan was "All Power to the TUC General Council", hand everything to the very people who had no intention of fighting. It put "unity" above everything, even unity with leaders who were sitting on their hands. And at the decisive moment, it didn't give the strike a clear political aim of its own, it asked for a "general agreement," not for workers' power. It became, in effect, a cheerleader for the left leaders, instead of an independent force pushing and, where necessary, replacing them.

Why did it go wrong like that? Partly because the leadership of the Russian Revolution had, by then, begun to degenerate under Stalin, and it pressed the British Communists to keep cosy relations with the TUC lefts rather than criticise them honestly. The warnings of those, like Trotsky, who said the Communists must prepare "by a ruthless criticism of all the leading staff of the British Labour movement", those warnings were ignored. And after the defeat, the TUC's gratitude to the Minority Movement was to ban it.

Now. Why am I telling a 2026 audience all this in such detail?

Because we have, today, the closest thing this country has had to a Minority Movement since, the National Shop Stewards Network.

The Shop Stewards Network was founded in 2007, on the initiative of the rail union the RMT and its late, great general secretary Bob Crow. It is, exactly like the Minority Movement, a cross-union, rank-and-file network. It brings together shop stewards, reps and activists from every union into permanent contact with one another, to spread solidarity, share what works, strengthen democracy and accountability inside the unions, and fight for the militant action that's needed. Nine national unions support it, and the Socialist Party plays a leading role within it, as the Marxist organising core, just as the Communists did a century ago.

The strike wave of 2022 and 2023, the biggest in this country since before most of us were born, put the general strike back on the agenda for the first time in decades. When Mick Lynch said "the working class is back," it was true. And we are not in a lull now. The Labour government, and let's be clear, it is a thoroughly pro-business government, is pushing austerity. It was elected with a majority of over 160 and it still has not repealed the Tory anti-union laws it promised to scrap. The Birmingham bin workers have been on indefinite strike for over a year against a Labour council. Resident doctors, university staff, council workers, the disputes keep coming. And on top of it, the far right is once again trying to split the working class by scapegoating migrants, which is why the Shop Stewards Network is calling on the trade unions to lead the fight against it, work many of us are directly involved in.

So the question the Minority Movement got wrong is our question now. And the Shop Stewards Network is consciously trying to get the answer right. It does not just hand power upwards and hope. It builds from the bottom. It places concrete demands on the leaders, right and left. Every year it lobbies the TUC Congress; last year the demand was to name the date for a weekend demonstration against Labour austerity. In the past it has demanded coordinated action, and played a major role in the 2011 public sector general strike over pensions, where over 2 million workers were on strike. It fights for fighting policies, for a fifteen-pound minimum wage, for sweeping away the anti-union laws, and for officials who are regularly elected, subject to recall, and paid a worker's wage, so they can never become a comfortable caste sitting on top of the movement. And the Shop Stewards Network's own conference this Saturday, which everyone should come to, is being built around the centenary of 1926 and around that miners' slogan, "Not a penny off the pay, not a minute on the day." The movement is reaching back to 1926 deliberately, to arm itself for what's ahead.

Posing the question, and answering it

So in conclusion, 1926 proved, beyond all argument, the immense, society-stopping power of the working class. When it moves as a class, nothing on earth can run without its say-so. That power is just as real today, in a different economy, with different industries, but just as real.

But 1926 proved something else, just as important. A general strike poses the question of power. It does not, by itself, answer it. It asks: which class will run society? And to actually answer that question, to take society into the hands of the people who run it every day, you need two things the workers of 1926 did not have. You need a clear socialist programme. And you need a mass party, with a tested leadership, that is prepared to fight for it, and prepared to tell the truth about leaders who won't.

That is the unfinished business of 1926. And the building of it is the most important task in front of us. It runs through the Shop Stewards Network. It runs through the fight, which the Socialist Party is helping to lead right now, for a new mass party of the working class, because the industrial movement, however strong, will always need its own political voice. And it runs through the building of a revolutionary party, the Socialist Party, to carry that programme into the struggles ahead.

A worker in 1926, watching the leaders surrender a winning strike, said to one of them: "Next time, we'll win without you." That is our slogan for the battles ahead. Let's learn the lessons that generation paid for in hunger and defeat. Let's build the rank and file. Let's build the party. So that next time, we win.

Reply notes

A reply responds to the floor, so this was a toolkit rather than a script: the substance and the one fact or quote worth delivering on each point that was likely to come up, to be picked from live.

"Surely the working class just wasn't ready, wasn't strong enough?" This is the most important myth to demolish, so take it head-on. The strength was overwhelming, solid, growing, with the state's strike-breaking collapsing and the middle class wavering. Lenin set out four conditions for a revolutionary situation; in 1926 three of them clearly existed: the rulers split and unable to carry on the old way, the middle layers not solidly behind them, the workers in revolt and reaching for an alternative. The fourth condition, a mass party and leadership ready to take power, was the one missing. It wasn't the workers who weren't ready. It was the people at the top.

"But general strikes are illegal now, what about the anti-union laws?" Two-part answer. First: laws have never been the decisive thing. The 1920 Emergency Powers Act didn't stop 1926. In the early 1970s, Heath's anti-union law jailed five London dockers, and a strike that erupted from below freed them and made the law a dead letter. Mass defiance makes bad laws inoperable. Second: this is precisely why the Shop Stewards Network fights to sweep the anti-union laws off the books, and why it's a scandal that Labour, with a 160-plus majority, still hasn't repealed the laws it promised to scrap. We use whatever legal protections exist, with tactics like disaggregated ballots, but we don't let the laws veto action the movement needs.

"Wouldn't it be more revolutionary to call for an all-out, unlimited general strike right now?" No, and this is a question of Marxist seriousness, not radical-sounding phrases. Trotsky's point: calling an all-out general strike "requires a painstaking Marxist accounting of all the concrete circumstances." Sometimes a general strike frightens the rulers into concessions, as in Russia in 1905. But if an all-out strike develops and the working class can't take power, because the party and programme aren't there yet, the ruling class can inflict a defeat it takes years to recover from. That's the lesson of 1926 itself. Our job is to weigh the actual stage of the struggle and name the next concrete step. At this stage in Britain that step is the demand for a 24-hour general strike, which even on its own would be a huge advance, and slogans like "all strike together" that point in that direction.

"Aren't you just blaming a few bad leaders? Won't new leaders sell out too?" It's not about personalities, it's about programme and structures of accountability. Jimmy Thomas wasn't uniquely wicked; he was the product of a layer that had grown comfortable at the top of the apparatus and feared the members more than the bosses. The answer isn't to find nicer individuals and trust them, that was the Minority Movement's mistake, "All Power to the General Council." The answer is to build a conscious, organised rank and file that places demands on all leaders, holds them to account, and can replace them, plus the basic safeguards: regular re-election of officials, the right of recall, and a worker's wage so no one gets bought off by the comforts of office. That's structural insurance against the next Thomas.

"Can't we just get the Tories, or Starmer, to deliver? Isn't a Labour government the answer?" Getting rid of a rotten government is welcome, and millions want it. But, as Mick Lynch himself put it, "Starmer's Labour could be another version of the Tories." New Labour was remade into an openly pro-capitalist party; Starmer refuses even to back energy nationalisation, which two-thirds of the public support, because his priority is proving to big business that he's "their man." A Labour government managing capitalism in a crisis will be driven to attack workers, and the Birmingham bin strike, against a Labour council, is the living proof. So industrial action alone, however heroic, hits a ceiling. The industrial struggle needs its own political voice. That's why the fight for a new mass workers' party, which the Socialist Party is helping to lead right now, including through Trade Unionists for a New Party, is not a side issue. It's the missing fourth condition.

"Is the Shop Stewards Network really comparable to the Minority Movement? It's far smaller." True on scale, and that cuts the opposite way to how the objection assumes. The Minority Movement was huge, a quarter of all trade unionists, and it still failed, because its method was wrong: it tailed the leaders instead of pressing and replacing them. So size was never the decisive factor; method was. The Shop Stewards Network is smaller today, but it's consciously built on the right method: building from below, placing demands on right and left leaders alike, fighting for accountability, and giving the movement a political generalisation. Get the method right as the struggle grows, and the numbers can follow fast. If pushed on the Communist Party's size in 1926: about 4,000 members, small, yes, but with the right programme it could have come out of the strike many times larger and shifted the consciousness of the whole class. Smallness is not an excuse for the wrong strategy.

"There's a danger in counterposing 'rank-and-file work' to standing candidates and pressuring leaders." A sharp point worth affirming if it comes up. It's a false choice. Even "pure" rank-and-file organising generates leaders; the only questions are what they stand for, how they're held to account, and how they can be replaced. To refuse to stand fighting candidates for union positions, or to refuse to place demands on the left officials, is just to abstain from the battle over the leadership of the workers' own organisations. We do both: build the rank and file and contest the leadership, with the rank and file keeping the leaders under pressure.

"Okay, what do we actually do on Monday morning?" Bring it down to earth, because people came for this. Concretely: affiliate your union branch or trades council to the Shop Stewards Network and build it where you are; revive your local trades council as a fighting body; back the live disputes, Birmingham, the doctors, the universities, with real solidarity; take the demand for coordinated action and a 24-hour general strike into your union and to the TUC; throw yourself into the fight against the far right and against austerity; and, the decisive one, join the Socialist Party and help build the party and the programme that 1926 proved the movement cannot do without.

The three points to land

  1. The power is real and it is ours. 1926, and 2022 to 2023, proved it. Society runs on the working class, then and now.
  2. A general strike poses the question of power; it doesn't answer it. For the answer you need a socialist programme and a mass party with a leadership prepared to fight, and to tell the truth about leaders who won't.
  3. The lesson is a live task, not history. Build the rank and file through the Shop Stewards Network; fight for a new mass workers' party; build the Socialist Party. So that next time, we win.
