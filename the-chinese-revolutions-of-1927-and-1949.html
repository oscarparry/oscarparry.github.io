The Chinese revolutions of 1927 and 1949

10 July 2026 ChinaHistoryMarxismRevolution

A leadoff written for a CWI summer school. Two revolutions, one question: at each stage, what would you have done as a Marxist standing there, with the information available at the time?

Written as a forty-minute speaking script for a Committee for a Workers' International summer school, and never published as an article. The timing cues and pre-cut notes have been taken out; the three passages I had held in reserve for a generous chair are kept at the end. Everything else is as it was delivered.

Comrades. The two greatest events of the twentieth century were both revolutions, and both broke out in countries the schemas said were unready: Russia in October 1917, China in October 1949. However, to understand the victory of 1949 we have to begin with a defeat, the revolution of 1925 to 1927, and behind that defeat, the collapse of an empire. The method is that of Trotsky's History of the Russian Revolution: not fixed categories but living processes, the outcome open at each stage until real forces closed it.

And hold one question in front of you throughout: at each stage, what would you have done as a Marxist standing there, with the information available at the time? At almost every turn there were Chinese communists of the founding generation who gave the answers we would defend today, and who paid for them with prison, exile and worse. I will come back to them.

1911: the empire falls, nothing is solved

Start with 1911. China entered the twentieth century with a dynastic order stretching back some three thousand years. The last dynasty, the Qing, were Manchus, conquerors whom much of China never ceased to regard as foreign; resentment of alien rule was in the fabric before a single European gunboat arrived. When the gunboats came, in the Opium Wars, that feeling fused with a sharper hatred of the Western powers and later Japan, who forced the country open and carved out spheres of influence. China kept formal independence mainly because the robbers would not let one another swallow the prize whole. Anti-imperialism was not imported; it grew out of the pores of the society.

Onto that old order the world market dropped the newest capitalism. Isaacs, in The Tragedy of the Chinese Revolution, the book to put in every comrade's hands, gives the picture in one sentence:

On the fringes of big Chinese cities the shadows of lofty factory chimneys fall across fields still tilled with wooden ploughs.

Harold Isaacs, The Tragedy of the Chinese Revolution

Combined and uneven development in one image: modern mills and banks on top of a countryside where the peasant was crushed between rent, usury and taxes.

In October 1911 a garrison mutiny at Wuchang set off the avalanche; within months the dynasty was gone. The central figure of the republic was Sun Yat-sen, a sincere and courageous nationalist, twenty years an exile, whose Three People's Principles made him the moral centre of the awakening. Inaugurated provisional president on the first of January 1912, he lasted six weeks: with neither an army nor a class base, he handed power to Yuan Shikai, the strongest general of the old regime, who crushed the infant parliament, tried to make himself emperor and failed. China broke apart into the fiefdoms of the warlords; keep that in mind, China in this period was not a unified country.

Why did the fall of an empire change so little? Because 1911 was a bourgeois revolution without a revolutionary bourgeoisie. The monarch had gone; no class had arrived capable of taking his place. The landlords still stood on the neck of the peasantry; the merchants had grown rich as brokers of foreign capital; the industrial capitalists who did emerge were born fused with both, the same family owning the mill, holding village land, banking through the concessions. Such a class could quarrel with imperialism over the size of its share; however, it could not wage war to the end against landlordism and imperialism, for that would be war against itself. There is the class origin of the Kuomintang.

So the classic tasks of the bourgeois-democratic revolution remained unperformed. We say there were four. First, resolving the national question: unification and the breaking of imperialist domination. Second, establishing free labour: dissolving the feudal and semi-feudal bonds tying the producer to landlord, usurer and clan. Third, solving the land question, which capitalism anywhere achieves partially at best. Fourth, basic democratic rights. Every serious force claimed these tasks; the question of the century was which class could perform them.

By the mid-1920s a new candidate existed: some two million industrial workers, ten million more in transport and handicrafts, in a nation of four hundred million. A drop in the ocean numerically; however, it stood at the nerve centres on which warlords and powers alike depended, hardened by twelve to sixteen hour shifts and child apprenticeship. The lesson of 1917 was that such a class could lead: in a backward country the democratic and socialist tasks would be telescoped into one revolution, the workers at the head of the peasant millions. That is the permanent revolution, and China was its greatest test.

A party of thirteen delegates, a party of merchants

The Russian revolution detonated inside that situation. In 1919 Versailles handed Shandong to Japan, and on the fourth of May the students of Beijing came onto the streets; May Fourth became a national force in June when the Shanghai workers struck. Out of the ferment came Marxist circles and the Socialist Youth Corps in Beijing, through which a young Hunanese library assistant named Mao Zedong passed. The Comintern sent emissaries; Lenin followed the work closely. In July 1921, thirteen delegates representing perhaps fifty members founded the Communist Party of China in Shanghai, finishing on a hired boat on a lake as the police closed in. Mao was one of the thirteen; Chen Duxiu was elected leader in his absence. Do not picture a mass force: its early strength was borrowed from the authority of October and the militancy of the young working class. And note its composition: most members, Mao above all, had come to communism through the national struggle, anti-imperialists first, Marxists second. In Lenin's dying years that nationalism was never seriously challenged; the policy that came instead flattered it.

Now the other party. The Kuomintang, founded by Sun in 1912, was a national movement of the propertied and educated classes, merchants, gentry, officers, students, something like the Congress party in India: anti-imperialist in speech, propertied in substance. Sun appealed for years to Britain and America and was ignored; the power that answered was Soviet Russia. From 1923 the alliance was formalised, Borodin arrived, Soviet money and arms rebuilt the Kuomintang, and in 1924 the Whampoa academy was founded to give it an army. Its commandant is worth pausing on: Chiang Kai-shek, son of a salt merchant, trained in Japanese military schools, seasoned in the Shanghai world where the stock exchange and the Green Gang underworld shade into one another, sent to Moscow in 1923 and returned with a cold appreciation of Bolshevik methods and no attachment to Bolshevik aims.

Was any of this wrong in principle? No. Lenin's theses at the Second Congress in 1920 had drawn the line precisely: support the national movements against imperialism, however:

The Communist International must enter into a temporary alliance with bourgeois democracy in the colonial and backward countries, but should not merge with it, and should under all circumstances uphold the independence of the proletarian movement even if it is in its most embryonic form.

Lenin, Draft Theses on the National and Colonial Questions, June 1920

In the same fight Lenin warned of the need to wage determined war on attempts to cloak bourgeois liberation movements in a communist garb. The context is usually dropped: he was not forbidding alliances, he was forbidding false colours. The Bolsheviks had shown the method in 1917, fighting Kornilov shoulder to shoulder with Kerensky's troops while giving Kerensky no political support: use the ally as a footrest against the main enemy of the hour, and never mistake the footrest for the road.

A temporary bloc with Sun's movement, conceivably even a spell of work inside its loose structures, was therefore defensible. However, that is not what was done. In 1922 the Comintern's delegate Maring summoned the central committee to West Lake at Hangzhou and imposed entry into the Kuomintang against the initial judgement of the Chinese leadership; by 1923 the line was that the Kuomintang must lead the national revolution. Trotsky opposed entry from 1923 onward. The tactic hardened into a strategy, the strategy into a theology, and one Comintern delegate put the doctrine in words Chen Duxiu never forgot:

The present period is a period in which the Communists should do the coolie service for the Kuo Min Tang.

Comintern delegate, quoted in Chen Duxiu, Appeal to All the Comrades of the Chinese Communist Party, December 1929

Communists came to sit in the Kuomintang's own leading bodies: Mao himself was an alternate member of its central executive and for a period ran its propaganda department and its peasant institute. The two parties were not allied; one was dissolved into the other.

1925 to 1927: the revolution and the executioner

Then the class moved. In May 1925, after a communist worker was killed in a Japanese-owned mill and British-officered police shot demonstrators in Shanghai, the city rose in a general strike, and the movement leapt to Canton and Hong Kong, where a strike and boycott of British trade held for fifteen months. The Canton strikers built two thousand elected pickets, a delegate council, kitchens, hospitals, courts: here, in embryo, were soviets, created by Chinese workers out of their own experience. The peasant associations grew into the millions. The party grew with the wave, from roughly a thousand members at the start of 1925 towards several tens of thousands by early 1927; set against the movement it led, however, it remained small, its authority resting to a great degree on the borrowed prestige of October.

In Hunan the peasant movement found its chronicler inside the party. Mao, sent to inspect it in early 1927, reported:

In a very short time ... several hundred million peasants will rise like a mighty storm, like a hurricane, a force so swift and violent that no power, however great, will be able to hold it back. ... a revolution is not a dinner party ...

Mao, Report on an Investigation of the Peasant Movement in Hunan, March 1927

Mark his position: he argued for the poor peasantry to be unleashed against the landlords at the moment the official line required them to be restrained, so as not to alarm the landlord-officers of the army. On that point the young Mao stood in contradiction to the Stalinist line, and closer to the truth.

In July 1926 the Northern Expedition set out from Canton against the warlords, the whole CCP thrown into it as commissars and organisers. It advanced less by military genius than by revolution: railway workers sabotaged the warlords' communications, peasants rose ahead of the columns; by winter it stood on the Yangtze. An instrument had been forged, and everything turned on one question: which class would wield it?

Chiang showed his hand a year early: in March 1926 in Canton, communists arrested, strike pickets disarmed, then decrees removing communists from leading Kuomintang posts, forbidding criticism of Sun Yat-senism, demanding the CCP's membership lists. Chen Duxiu recorded the response in four words: all these we accepted. Chen proposed leaving; Moscow denounced him. Then a detail a novelist would reject: Chiang pressed for the Kuomintang to be admitted into the Comintern, and it was accepted as a sympathising party, one vote cast against, Trotsky's; and while preparing the blow of April 1927, as Trotsky noted, Chiang took care to exchange portraits with Stalin.

In preparing himself for the role of an executioner, he wanted to have the cover of world communism, and he got it.

Trotsky, Stalin and the Chinese Revolution, 1930

Why would Chiang turn? Class position, not personal wickedness. The official theory held that the yoke of imperialism welded the progressive classes together, the famous bloc of four classes, so the revolution must stay within limits the ally could accept. Trotsky answered in the weeks the blow was falling:

The revolutionary struggle against imperialism does not weaken, but rather strengthens the political differentiation of the classes. ... everything that brings the oppressed and exploited masses of the toilers to their feet, inevitably pushes the national bourgeoisie into an open bloc with the imperialists.

Trotsky, The Chinese Revolution and the Theses of Comrade Stalin, May 1927

Behind the algebraic slogan of the 'democratic dictatorship of workers and peasants', which Trotsky attacked in these months, one question was left blank: which class leads? Life filled it in with Chiang.

In March 1927 the workers of Shanghai rose at the approach of the nationalist army, between half a million and eight hundred thousand of them with about a hundred and fifty pistols, defeated the warlord garrison and took the greatest industrial city in Asia before a single nationalist soldier entered it. Then they handed it, organised and confident, to Chiang's army, because their leaders told them the army was theirs. On the fifth of April Stalin assured a Moscow meeting that the Kuomintang right would be used to the end, squeezed out like a lemon and flung away; the Comintern press had just declared the Kuomintang ill for lack of workers' and peasants' blood, which the communists must help infuse. Six days later the transfusion came. Before dawn on the twelfth of April, Green Gang gunmen in workers' clothes, armed through the foreign concessions, fell on the union halls with Chiang's soldiers; pickets were shot where they stood. Next morning a hundred thousand came out unarmed in protest; machine guns met the procession, the wounded finished with bayonets in the rain. Thousands died, and the white terror rolled across the country. For twenty-one days, a Comintern eyewitness admitted, the workers' government of Shanghai had shown complete inactivity: waiting for its ally.

Could it have gone otherwise?

the situation could still have been saved in Shanghai by receiving Chiang Kai-shek not as a liberator but as a mortal foe.

Trotsky, Stalin and the Chinese Revolution, 1930

The workers held the city; a sympathetic division offered to fight beside them and was turned away. What was missing was not strength but a policy permitting the strength to be used. Our running question, at its sharpest edge: soviets, the arming of the class, Chiang met as an enemy. Every element existed; the policy forbade them all.

Nor did the experiment stop. Moscow repeated it in Wuhan with the 'left' Kuomintang: two communist ministers administering restraint, strikes arbitrated, land seizures condemned, because the army's officers were landlords' sons. In May the risen peasants of Hunan were sabred by the thousand after the Horse Day massacre at Changsha; in July Wuhan expelled the communists, and the execution squads finished it. The counter-revolution killed tens of thousands within months, hundreds of thousands over the years of terror. In early 1927 workers were over half the party's membership; by 1930, under two per cent. A revolution had been beheaded and a workers' party severed from its class. That is the tragedy of the Chinese revolution: not that the workers were too weak, but that they were strong enough to win and were led, bound hand and foot, to the executioner.

After the massacre: three answers

Before the strategy, feel the moment, because Maoism is psychologically incomprehensible without it. You are twenty-five. You have watched your branch, your union and your leadership shot in batches by the ally you were ordered to serve; and the International that ordered it declares its line correct, making your founder, Chen Duxiu, the scapegoat. The rage, the grief, the hunger for arms: that was the raw material of everything that followed. The question was which politics would organise it.

The Comintern's answer was to prove its line correct by insurrection: Stalin turned, in Peng Shuzhi's phrase, from opportunism to adventurism. The Nanchang rising in August 1927; the Autumn Harvest risings, Mao commanding in Hunan; and in December the Canton Commune, staged in a city whose workers' movement was already broken, timed to serve the party congress in Moscow, and drowned in blood inside three days. Adventurism is opportunism turned inside out, and between them they finished in the cities what Chiang had begun.

Trotsky's answer, and the Chinese Opposition's, was of a different kind, and our running question has its least romantic, most instructive reply here. Retreat, said the Opposition, is a revolutionary policy when the class has been defeated. The second revolution had ended in crushing defeats and an interrevolutionary period had opened; however, the 1929 platform declared, the rise of a third revolution is inevitable, absolutely and completely grounded, in its words, in the conditions of the defeat of the second.

In the meantime: defensive struggles, the eight-hour day, the land, the democratic slogan of a constituent assembly; patient rebuilding in the cities, in the party's own class. And from Canton, Trotsky drew the deepest conclusion, in 1928:

the third Chinese revolution ... will not have a "democratic" period ... but it will be compelled from the very outset to effect the most decisive shake-up and abolition of bourgeois property in city and village.

Trotsky, Summary and Perspectives of the Chinese Revolution, June 1928

Hold that sentence: written in 1928, it is 1949 predicted twenty-one years in advance, down to the compulsion.

Who took that road? Chen Duxiu himself: the founder studied the Opposition's documents in disgrace, declared in 1929 that Trotsky had been right, and was expelled. What it cost him and the Chinese Trotskyists, Chiang's prisons, Mao's December 1952 round-up, Zheng Chaolin's twenty-seven years, is in the appendix below; it belongs to this story's honour roll.

And Mao? Describe his answer exactly, because the Maoist legend and the lazy anti-Maoist one both get it wrong. Mao did not deduce the peasant road from a book; he was driven onto it. When the cities were lost, the peasant movement he had organised in Hunan was what remained alive under his hands, and it protected him. The peasants were revolting for their lives, for food and land, and taking the land themselves; Mao stood with the poor and landless and argued for landlordism to be finished as a class. For refusing, in effect, to hold the peasantry back, and for the failure of the Autumn Harvest rising, he was dismissed from the Politburo, and for a period believed himself expelled from the party. Grasp the double character of that moment: in breaking from the attempt to restrain the peasant war, Mao was, in a certain sense, correct; in basing the party completely on the peasantry, severed from the working class, he planted the revolution in a class that could fight with immense heroism but could not rule collectively. He took the survivors up the Jinggang mountains, fused them with Zhu De's mutineers, and built peasant armies and base areas, the peasant association, not the workers' soviet, the model of power. His conclusion was burned in for life: political power grows out of the barrel of a gun.

However, Trotsky asked the question nobody in the official movement would ask, about the class character of the new armies:

For two or three years they live the lives of partisan commanders and commissars; they wage battles, seize territories, etc. They absorb the spirit of their environment. ... the revolutionary peasantry of China, in the person of its ruling stratum, seems to have appropriated to itself beforehand the political and moral capital which should by the nature of things belong to the Chinese workers. Isn't it possible that things may turn out so that all this capital will be directed at a certain moment against the workers?

Trotsky, Peasant War in China and the Proletariat, September 1932

File that question; 1949 and everything after is the answer. The comrades had been murdered; the working class, however, had not been destroyed, and by the early thirties its strikes were reviving. The decisive mistake was not that communists organised the peasants. It was that the party abandoned its own class, that the peasantry's power was never harnessed through the workers' movement, and that the military road, adopted from necessity, was made a virtue and a doctrine.

1927 to 1949: how a defeat became a victory

What changed between 1927 and 1949 to turn a beaten remnant into the master of China? Follow the chain. First the last adventures: Li Lisan's attempt in 1930 to seize the cities with peasant columns, Changsha held for days and drowned. Then Moscow's appointees took the leadership, and the Comintern sent in a German adviser, Otto Braun, to run the war against Chiang's fifth encirclement campaign, a million men behind a creeping ring of blockhouses. Braun's positional warfare broke the Jiangxi base, the soviet region governed since 1931, and in October 1934 the Red Army broke out west and north: the Long March. Show the map if you can; the alternative was extermination. Peng Shuzhi gives the ledger without romance:

... 90 percent of the Long March troops were also lost. The Long March began with three hundred thousand people, and when the march reached Yenan, only 30,000 were left.

Peng Shuzhi, introduction to Leon Trotsky on China

At the Zunyi conference in January 1935, with the Comintern's line discredited by its results, the commander whose guerrilla methods had at least kept armies alive took the military leadership. Mao's supremacy dates from there: from the wreckage of the Comintern's strategy, not from a congress of ideas. The march, nine in ten dead, became the founding myth of the People's Republic; and a movement decapitated once had learned never to be decapitated again. A great part of what the CCP became is in that sentence.

Second, Japan: Manchuria seized in 1931, the whole country invaded in 1937. Chiang's answer, internal pacification first, resistance afterwards, meant hunting the communists while appeasing the invader and collaborating with the powers whose concessions garrisoned his cities. The anti-Japanese rage of the people became the central political fact in China, and it condemned the Kuomintang. In December 1936 Chiang was kidnapped at Xi'an by his own Manchurian troops, made homeless by his non-resistance; Stalin, wanting China whole as a counterweight to Japan, ordered the CCP to secure his release and an alliance. The terms, as Peng Shuzhi records them: the CCP guaranteed Chiang's safety in return for his consent to lead the war, agreed to abolish the Red Army and the soviets in name, and gave up the agrarian revolution.

Terms matter here. A united front is a fighting agreement between class organisations, independence and criticism preserved; a popular front subordinates the workers' organisations to a bourgeois partner. The twenties entry was a popular front in extreme form, inside one party; 1937 was proclaimed under the new popular front line, and its content, giving up the agrarian revolution, matched. However, one thing differed from 1925, and it decided everything: Mao kept his own army, territory and administration, coming from an existing base as a power, not a supplicant; he had learned the military lesson of 1927 in his bones, whatever we say of the class lesson. That is why 1937 did not end as 1927 ended. The Kuomintang, for its part, could not fight the imperialist power devouring it, and could not refuse the alliance either.

And the war itself? Trotsky was asked exactly that:

Tomorrow he may again betray. It is possible. It is probable. It is even inevitable. But today he is struggling. Only cowards, scoundrels, or complete imbeciles can refuse to participate in that struggle.

Trotsky, On the Sino-Japanese War, September 1937

Participation in the war to the end; no political support to Chiang; preparation for the reckoning. That is the united front method applied to a national war.

Then the Second World War redrew everything in the CCP's favour. Japan shattered the Kuomintang's hold on the richest provinces while guerrilla bases spread behind the lines; the Kuomintang state rotted as it fought, hyperinflation eating the cities, Truman privately calling its leaders thieves, every damn one of them. The configuration of 1927 had inverted: the United States, war-weary, its Moscow alliance freezing into Cold War, would subsidise Chiang but not fight in China, while the Soviet Union stood in Manchuria and passed captured Japanese arms to the PLA. The breathing space the first revolution never had, its successor received in full.

The civil war came in 1946, as it had to; a revolution of this depth is always pregnant with civil war. Land reform resumed, and the peasant armies swelled with men who now had land to defend; Chiang's unpaid conscripts came over in whole formations; by 1948 and 1949 the PLA was taking the great cities, often without serious fighting. And the working class was not absent, whatever the legend says. From September 1945, as Vincent Kolo records, a magnificent strike wave swept every major city, 200,000 out in Shanghai alone, with the students pouring onto the streets beside the workers.

Here was 1925 stirring again. A party of the 1917 school would have thrown everything into it: generalised the strikes, fused them with the students, armed the pickets, built soviets. Instead, in Kolo's words, the CCP applied the brakes, urging the masses not to go to extremes; the strikes became a bargaining counter, and when the PLA entered the cities the workers were told to stay at their posts and keep production going. Sections of workers fought in the underground; the role assigned to the class by the leadership, however, was that of an orderly audience at its own liberation.

One more fact should be better known: power was taken against Moscow's advice. Stalin, fearing American intervention and content with a weak, divided China on his border, pressed the CCP in 1945 into coalition with Chiang; Mao complained openly in later years that Stalin had tried to prevent the Chinese revolution. He went to Chongqing, negotiated, and did the opposite. On taking power Mao was right and Stalin was wrong, and we say so without embarrassment, as our International said recently to the left parties of Nepal: with power within reach, at the least do what Mao did, and take it.

1949: the second revolution and the state it built

On the first of October 1949 Mao proclaimed the People's Republic from the Tiananmen gate. Measure the event before criticising it: the greatest revolutionary overturn since October 1917, in lives transformed the largest in history. Imperialism was thrown out bodily; China was unified; landlordism was destroyed in the greatest agrarian revolution ever carried out, in great part because the peasants took the land themselves. Set our four tasks against the result: the national question resolved in its essentials, though the American fleet kept Taiwan; the bondage of labour broken; the land question solved to an extent no bourgeois regime had approached. Three of the four tasks carried out, not by the bourgeoisie but against it: the permanent revolution confirmed, even if in caricature. The fourth, democratic rights, was carried out by no one, in neither bourgeois nor workers' form.

Because look at what took power. Not soviets; there were none. The CCP of 1949 was a party of peasant commanders and administrators formed for twenty years in the discipline of an army, and its programme, Mao's New Democracy, wrote the bloc of four classes into the state: the workers first on paper, then the peasants, the urban petty bourgeoisie and intelligentsia, and the 'national' bourgeoisie, with bourgeois ministers in the government and capitalist property protected for a whole stage. Understand the bloc in its own time: an established national bourgeoisie barely existed as an independent force in 1949; what actually gathered around the CCP was a bloc of radical nationalists who had fought Japan, and the majority of that generation's intelligentsia were themselves revolutionaries. As a description of the real alliance that won the war the formula had a content then that it cannot have now; when today's Maoists lift it out of that context and offer it to countries with mighty working classes and established bourgeoisies, they are attempting time travel.

However, taking power did not end the class struggle; it moved the struggle inside the new state. The classes assembled in the coalition, and represented inside the party and government, pulled at once in different directions, bourgeois ministers one way, workers and poor peasants another, the bureaucracy balancing above. It could not last: Korea, blockade, sabotage and the flight of capital forced the pace, and within three years the regime that had promised to protect capitalist property was expropriating it wholesale; by the mid-fifties landlordism and capitalism were liquidated. Peng Shuzhi wrote the epitaph of New Democracy:

under the irresistible pressure of objective conditions the CCP, in order to protect itself, was forced to yield to the laws of permanent revolution and nationalize the property of the bourgeoisie, thereby making China a workers' state.

Peng Shuzhi, introduction to Leon Trotsky on China

Trotsky's 1928 forecast was fulfilled almost to the letter; fulfilled, however, by a force and in a form he did not choose, and the form has governed everything since. The later convulsions are seeded here: when the classes inside the party and government struggled among themselves after victory, the leadership answered as its school had taught, from above, by campaign and purge. Without that contradiction nothing about the Cultural Revolution can be explained.

So what kind of state? Compare the two Octobers honestly. Russia 1917: soviets, elected and recallable, workers' control in the factories, a mass party alive with debate: the elements, in embryo, of a relatively healthy workers' state, degraded later, under isolation and the defeat of the European revolution, into the Stalinist machine. China 1949: a planned economy in due course, and gains we defend; however, from the first hour, no organ of workers' democracy at all. The new state was modelled on the Moscow of 1949, not the Petrograd of 1917.

whereas the Russian Revolution degenerated under unfavorable historical conditions, the Chinese Revolution was bureaucratically disfigured from the outset.

Vincent Kolo, 70 Years Since the Chinese Revolution, 2019

There, as Kolo says, is the key to Maoism's contradictions, important social gains alongside repression and dictatorial rule. Our tradition's older shorthand was a deformed workers' state; say disfigured, distorted, bureaucratised from birth, the vocabulary matters less than the substance: a state that planned production in the name of a class it excluded from power. In that birthmark lies the seed of everything after: the purges of yesterday's coalition partners, the Great Leap, the Cultural Revolution, in which Mao would lean on the workers against his rivals and then break their demands, Tiananmen in 1989, and the hybrid Bonapartist state balancing over Chinese society today.

The last time of asking: what would we have done in 1949? Take power? Yes, without hesitation, against Stalin's advice, with Mao. However: base it on the working class as it actually was, in movement, asking for exactly this; convoke congresses of workers', peasants' and soldiers' deputies; place the plan under the producers' control; link the new China to the international revolution. Nothing there is utopian; the strike wave had shown the class present and willing. It was policy, not possibility, that fixed the part the class was permitted to play.

Why this history is a weapon

Three last points. First, the anti-imperialist reflex of Chinese society is not a creation of CCP propaganda. The man who machine-gunned the Shanghai workers sat in Taipei until his death in 1975 as the recognised president of 'China', holding the UN seat into the 1970s, kept there by the American fleet. A century of gunboats and concessions, an invasion that killed tens of millions, and then that: the memory is material, and our programme has to speak to it, as the CCP's nationalism cynically does.

Second, the argument with Maoism today. We answer it historically, not by denying Mao's achievement. His strategy was the product of a specific defeat in a specific country: a working class beheaded by a policy Mao did not make, a peasantry in revolt for its life, an invading empire, a rotting regime. In that situation it conquered, and where the same door stands open, taking power is the part of his record we defend. However, whoever wants to universalise the strategy must universalise the price stamped on the state it built: the absence of workers' democracy, which is not an ornament of socialism but its mechanism.

Third, the thread back to our question. At every stage there was an answer, given at the time, in documents you can still read: Lenin's conditions in 1920; no dissolving into the Kuomintang in 1923; break with Chiang after the first coup in 1926; soviets, and Chiang met as a mortal foe, in 1927; patience and the cities after the defeat; workers' leadership of the peasant war in 1932; the united front without subordination in 1937; everything into the strike wave in 1945; power in 1949, based on the class that can rule collectively. The people who gave those answers, Trotsky, Chen Duxiu, Peng Shuzhi, Wang Fanxi, Zheng Chaolin and the hundreds whose names we do not have, paid in full. The third Chinese revolution confirmed their analysis in the negative, by showing what a revolution becomes when the working class is not at its head. The Chinese working class of today, potentially the most powerful on the planet, will confirm it in the positive; and when it moves it will need exactly what was missing between 1911 and 1949: an independent party of its own, workers' democracy at its core, internationalism in its bloodstream. Building the forces that fight for that is what this school is for.

Appendix: three passages held in reserve

The Chinese Trotskyists. Chen Duxiu, the founder, studied the Opposition's documents in disgrace, declared in 1929 that Trotsky had been right, and was expelled. Hundreds of the Chinese students at Moscow's Sun Yat-sen University came over too, and were deported or imprisoned; the story is in Gregor Benton's histories of Chinese Trotskyism. In 1931 the Opposition groups unified, with Chen as secretary; within weeks they were betrayed to the Kuomintang police, and Chen and Peng Shuzhi spent years in Chiang's prisons. Carry it to the end: in December 1952, three years after the victory, Mao's government arrested every Trotskyist it could find in one night's sweep, hundreds of the founding generation. Zheng Chaolin served twenty-seven years without trial and emerged in 1979 unbroken. There were people who answered our question correctly at every stage; that is what it cost them.

How the imperialists changed method. The imperialists had drawn their conclusion. Gunboats had been tried, British ships shelled Wanhsien in 1926, and the movement grew anyway; so the method changed to contacts, credits and a floated ten million dollar loan to the Canton government. The bankers and the settlements worked on the Kuomintang leaders from one side, the class fear of the Chinese bourgeoisie pressed from the other; in October 1926 the Canton government called off the Hong Kong strike without a single demand won.

On the peasant war. Our tradition never sneered at the peasant war. After the Paris Commune, Engels returned to the peasant question and reissued The Peasant War in Germany; Lenin carried the analysis to its conclusion, that the workers can and must lead the peasant millions; and Trotsky in 1932 chose his title deliberately: The Peasant War in China and the Proletariat. The peasant movement, he wrote there, is a mighty revolutionary factor, and he who forgets the dual nature of the peasantry is not a Marxist.
