The economy is natural

2 December 2022 EconomicsAnthropology

Written for AN456, Anthropology of Economy. The presentation of a capitalist world economy as natural is a discursive tool for holding the everyday political order of capitalism in place, and anthropology is well placed to take it apart.

Set question: 'The Economy is Natural.' Discuss.

In this essay I will detail how the idea of the economy as 'natural' and separated from society is a powerful discursive tool used to uphold the everyday political order of capitalism. I will show the history of the economy as a separate sphere was predicated on the creation of the 'false commodities' of land, labour and money (Polanyi), and how the modern idea of a national economy arose during a mid-twentieth century crisis of economic representation (Mitchell). I will then argue that the idea of a global economy is used to launch a 'new imperialism' in which huge amounts of profit are created by 'accumulation by dispossession' (Harvey). I will also trace how these processes are ongoing through ethnographic examples (Li, Mintz, Bourgois, Parry).

Polanyi argues that up to the end of feudalism, the economic sphere was simply an offshoot of the social sphere. The production and distribution of goods and services were organised around a combination of symmetry, centricity and householding. Decisions about what to produce and how, were made according to the values of religion, kinship, magic and subjugated to the political order. He argues that the industrial revolution led to a revolution in the production of goods, for now the primary motive was material gain. Polanyi traces the formulation of a society based on a 'self-regulating market' to the creation of the 'fictional commodities' of land, labour and money. He argues that the processes of enclosure of common land in England forced the rural population into wage work in the towns. The rural villagers could no longer supplement their income with subsistence agriculture, so their 'choice' was to work in factories under brutal conditions or face starvation. Thus society was remade from a feudal system of peasants and yeomen into one guided solely by the 'invisible hand' of the market.

Li details how this process of change, far from being a long-ago occurrence, is still shaping the lives of many rural populations worldwide. Her ethnography focuses on the Lauje highlanders of Indonesia, who transition from subsistence agriculture on land held in common to producing cash crops for exports, including cacao. The more permanent nature of the cacao crop means that whoever plants the tree owns the land it is on, leading to massive privatisation of land previously shared by kinship networks. This quickly creates a class of successful landlord farmers who then begin buying up more land, leaving many highlanders with no access to land. These less fortunate highlanders are forced to begin wage work, however there is not enough work to go round and some highlanders are left at 'land's end,' with no income from land or wage-work. This illustrates the absurdity of calling 'the economy' natural, by introducing capitalistic relations the entire Lauje society is remade in the space of a few decades. The Lauje case also disrupts the view of enclosures as a top-down state-backed occurrence, here the farmers themselves make the choice to enclose their common land.

Mitchell describes how the idea of a national economy can be traced back to Keynes' General Theory. Aristotle defined economy as the efficient management of households, while even Adam Smith still used the word to mean thrifty. Mirowski argues that the formulation of 'scientific economics' as a discipline borrowed much of its language and imagery from physics, including the tendency to swap analysis of events for mathematical formula, and to accept these formula as a form of proof. Mitchell argues that it was the collapse of the gold standard and the international monetary system, as well as the end of direct colonial rule, that led to the formulation of the idea of a national economy. The new national economy was represented by a series of aggregates, including Gross Domestic Product, which its inventor Kuznets warned may give too much weight to 'that vague entity called the national economy.' Mitchell also argues that a major conceptual shift was a switch from a static model of the economy to a dynamic one, the new economy could 'grow' without altering its physical limits in the border of the nation state. This new idea of a dynamic economy also gave birth to a neo-colonial project of 'development,' whereby countries on the periphery of the global capitalist system were chained to the core economies in the name of 'progress and prosperity.'

Mintz's ethnographic work is based in the sugar plantations of Puerto Rico. He follows the life of Don Taso, a 'worker in the cane.' Taso details the brutal nature of work in the cane fields, irregular and lowly paid, he had to travel constantly to find new work. He talks about the blacklisting of Socialist party members who were denied work, illustrating how the political order of capitalism is upheld by 'the economy.' Taso references the terraforming effects of the plantation on the landscape, here, rather than the economy being 'natural,' nature is remade in service of the economy. Mintz details how the rise of the global sugar trade was built on the back of the transatlantic slave trade, and how it was predicated on the manufacturing of a desire for sugar in Europe, especially England, where it became a staple good in every kitchen. Thus the global economy can be seen to be a constructed entity based on the subjugation of colonial and neo-colonial workers, far from a natural occurrence.

Mitchell argues that two prominent spheres are defined as external to the economy; the state and the household. The state is seen to define and regulate the economy, but Mitchell argues that it is embroiled in creating the economy at every stage, without the state, the economy would not exist. The apparent separation of state and economy is a discursive effect which is used to uphold the political reality of capitalism, and lend an air of inevitability to economic decisions. Harris argues that the view of the household as a private, separate sphere, free from commodity exchange, containing a 'natural economy' of kinship relations, is an illusion based on the abstract concept of money. Households are inherently intertwined in a system of general commodity exchange. Harris states that the view of the household as a separate sphere to society is so strong in Western thought that it is often projected onto radically different structures of household relations in other societies. Viewing the household as external to the economy fails to recognise that unpaid domestic labour is a key in the reproduction of the labour force, the 'external' domestic sphere is vital in the creation of the economy.

Harvey argues that a global capitalist system will always produce crises of overaccumulation, and seek 'spatio-temporal' fixes. He argues that inherent in the system is the tendency for capital to create new spaces of accumulation, to replace existing social relations with capitalistic ones involving private property and monetary exchange. This leads to a 'new imperialism' where a large source of profit is derived from 'accumulation by dispossession.' Parry has detailed how the liberalisation of the Indian economy in 1991 has radically altered the life of workers in the Bhilai Steel Plant in central India. Greater than the manual/non-manual divide there is now a large disparity between those with contract labour, termed nakri, and those with temporary wage work, termed kam. The changing nature of the economy has created two classes that see themselves as radically different to each other and experience massive differences in income, nakri workers are in the top quarter of national incomes in India while kam workers are in the bottom fifth. Thus the definition of the national economy can be seen to be in constant flux, changing from Nehru's vision of a 'socialist pattern of society' to Modi's current neo-liberal regime. This is part of a wider process of 'liberalisation' whereby a 'global precariat' of insecure and 'flexible' wage labour is used to generate massive profits for transnational corporations, most of them American.

Finally, the economy, rather than being natural, is implicit in creating 'natural' differences of ethnicity as outlined by Bourgois in his ethnography of a United Fruit Company banana plantation in Central America. Bourgois details how two groups of Amerindians articulate with the economic order of the plantation in massively different ways, the Kuna use their corporate and cultural structure to secure themselves a position higher in the occupational hierarchy than the Guaymí, who are placed at the bottom of the economic hierarchy. The difference in ethnicity between the two groups is leveraged by the company to divide the labour force, creating an air that the ethnic hierarchy is 'natural' due to the massive racism and public humiliation that the Guaymí are subjected to.

In conclusion, there is nothing natural about a capitalistic global economy, rather the entire rest of society is remade to service its imagined needs; land, labour and money are treated as real commodities to be bought and sold, the state is subjugated to transnational capital, ethnic differences are exacerbated, the patriarchal household is presented as natural to reproduce the labour force, and a global precariat of workers in appalling conditions is created, all to generate wealth for the investor 1%. The presentation of the economy as 'natural' is used to justify this arrangement and shut down any form of political debate, as the economy is subject to 'natural laws' that cannot be challenged. A key part of challenging the global hegemonic order of capital will be arguing against the economy as 'natural' and revealing the interests of those who present it as such.
