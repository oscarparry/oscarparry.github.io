The uses of the primitive

13 November 2022 Anthropology

Written for AN404, Theory and Ethnography. Ghassan Hage wants to salvage a primitivist anthropology for radical politics. The trouble is that the primitive has always been a pawn in somebody else's political project.

First published in coursework essay, MSc Social Anthropology, LSE

Set question: Although the concept of the 'primitive' has been subject to much critique, we should cultivate what Ghassan Hage (2012: 304) has recently described as the 'ethos of primitivist anthropology'. Discuss.

In this essay I will argue that Hage's call to cultivate the 'ethos of primitivist anthropology' (2012: 304), falls into a long history of treating people viewed as culturally 'other' as a tool in a Western political project. The idea of the 'primitive' is romanticised and twisted to fit any purpose, often finding "its characteristic outlet in social criticism" (Stocking 1976: 31). Torgovnick writes that Western fascination with the supposed 'primitives' is actually a discourse about "some essential part of the self" (1990: 34). I will argue with Trouillot that a critical anthropology requires a move beyond the "self-indulgent condemnation of traditional techniques and tropes" and requires "a reappraisal of the symbolical organization upon which the anthropological discourse is premised" (2003: 9).

To start I will trace the formation of the foundation of anthropology as a discipline and how it has come to occupy what Trouillot terms 'the savage slot' (2003: 7). Fabian has demonstrated that it was the secularization of the Judeo-Christian notion of history during the Renaissance that laid the foundations for the establishment of anthropology as an autonomous discipline (2014: 2). Moravia details how the idea and practice of "travel as science" was definitively established toward the middle of the eighteenth century (1973: 125-132). The new traveller "criticized the philosophes: the reality of lived experience and of things seen was now opposed to a reality distorted by preconceived ideas" (Moravia 1976: 963), illustrating the move from a religious interpretation of the world to a new faith in 'reason.' Fabian shows us the evolutionist tendencies of early anthropology, which created "scientific" categorizations like "savage," "barbaric," and "civilized" as stages of historical development (Bunzl 2014: ix). This defined the 'primitive' as not only spatially but also temporally distant from the West; "The philosophical traveller, sailing to the ends of the earth, is in fact travelling in time; he is exploring the past; every step he makes is the passage of an age" (Degérando 1969 [1800]: 63). Fabian argues that this "allochronism" (2014: 32) established the West as the pinnacle of universal human progress, establishing axiomatic truths of colonial 'development' which persist today.

This view of the 'primitive' as temporally separated from the West is not confined to anthropology. Torgovnick has illustrated Freud's evolutionist views; he says the 'primitive' represents "a necessary stage of development through which every race has passed" (1950: 29). She brings to light Jung's temporal distancing of Africa, for him the continent is a place of "eternal beginning," the "immemorially known." It gives "the most intense sentiment of returning to the land of my youth." Torgovnick goes on to define several recurring tropes in Western thought that are associated with the 'primitive'; "primitives are like children," "primitives are our untamed selves, our id forces - libidinous, irrational, violent, dangerous. Primitives are mystics, in tune with nature, part of its harmonies. Primitives are free. Primitives exist at the 'lowest cultural levels', we occupy the highest" (1990: 9-11).

Hage believes, however, that a "primitivist anthropology" can be salvaged from its colonial complicity (2012). He has three main arguments supporting his idea; his first is that we must learn from the "people outside modernity" in order to construct an "alter-modernity." By exposing ourselves to different "cosmologies" we can enhance the "radical political imaginary" of what is socially and culturally possible. He cites a lecture given by Lévi-Strauss: "Anthropologists are here to witness that the manner in which we live, the values that we believe in, are not the only possible ones" (2011: 51). His second argument is that there always exists the possibility of "another reality" within our minds, tracing out a history of thinkers who locate the 'primitive' within the psyche. He cites Lévy-Bruhl: "there is a mystical mentality that is more easily demarcated and therefore more easily observable among the 'primitives' than in our societies, but it is present in every human mind" (1985: 131). His third argument is that we should apply a "primitive ethos" to all anthropological work, searching for "minor realities" that contain "inaccessible" and "opaque" spaces, as "radical alterity is present everywhere."

Hage envisions anthropology's most radical potential as a contribution to 'critical thinking.' He argues that any form of critical thinking "inevitably associates itself with a politics that breaks routinized adhesion to a given social order." Critical history makes us reflect on the way history has shaped us, while critical sociology helps us view the causal power of social structure and social forces. Critical sociology produces processes of "denaturalization" and "de-fatalization" (Bourdieu 1990: 15) whereby recognising social spaces as "social constructs" and "objects of struggle" can induce hope in the possibility of social change. The critical role of anthropology for Hage is to prove that "we can be radically different from what we are," making the possibility of being 'other' act as a force in our lives (2012: 286-290).

Hage defines two types of radical politics, an "anti" and an "alter". He links his ideas to a shift from the "anti" Marxist politics guided by a "political imaginary that highlighted the anti-capitalist analytic moment over the imaginative labour of conceiving what a communist society would entail," towards an "alter" politics, where there is a "higher awareness of an intimate relation between means and ends." He believes that the rise of Marxism at the expense of the "anarchists" and "utopian socialists" such as Fourier and Saint-Simon reflected a socio-historical situation where the "anti" side of the binary dominated radical politics. His view is that the study of "radical cultural alterity" can contribute to the "imaginative labour" of envisaging an alternative society (2012: 292). I argue with Rosenblatt that both past and contemporary uses of the 'other' derive their salience from deep rooted Western ideas about what 'society' is, in particular the Book of Genesis and Enlightenment theories of 'the social contract.' These myths constitute an imaginative space in which a valorisation of 'the primitive' can be an attack on the structures of power that are in place (1997: 293). Hage is using his definition of the 'primitive' as a pawn in his political project.

Hage's idea of studying 'people outside of modernity' in order to feel "haunted ... by what we could be" (2012: 290) can fall into the trap of treating these groups as a "people without history" (Wolf 1982). The idea that any other 'cosmology' is possible to cultivate within ourselves fails to recognise the genealogy of both the 'others' beliefs but also our own. Hage turns a blind eye to the very specific historical and cultural development that has led any society to be the way it is. Although it is true that the present is created by a past which is selectively read from the created present, it is also true that the present has a historical and existential connection with the past. When people "invent" new political movements, they do so in ways that are meaningful to themselves and others, out of existing practices, and with purposes that were shaped by a particular historical experience (Rosenblatt 1997: 291).

Hage argues that "capitalist modernity" opens upon "new-worlds" of study, that "there is always an outside of a system of intelligibility, of governmentality, of domestication, of instrumental reason ... etc" (2012: 305-306). This falls directly into Lyotard's definition of postmodernism, "Postmodernism builds on this revelation of the sudden disappearance of established rules, foundational judgments, and known categories" (1986: 33). Trouillot argues that viewing the collapse of a "world of universals" as revelatory is a Western-specific phenomenon and has significance only within the boundaries of a Western reading of world history (2003: 11). Lyotard's statement that postmodernism seeks a "psychoanalytic therapeutic" from the "modern neurosis" (Lyotard 1986: 125-6), has clear links to Hage's view that "our otherness is always dwelling within us" (2012: 289), and by cultivating it we can remedy society's ills. Hage constructs the 'primitive' as the alter ego of the West, to further a utopian project in imaginary correspondence with the 'real' conditions of life 'outside modernity.'

Hage's distinction between the reader and the 'other' helps to reinforce the illusory binary which is a by-product of European modernity itself. The idea that there is such a thing as a 'radical cultural alterity' is a fictional bifurcation, which Trouillot has convincingly shown is rooted in the manufacturing of anthropology as a discursive field (2003). By cultivating the 'ethos of primitivist anthropology' as Hage describes it, we are reinforcing anthropology's much maligned role as a study of the 'other' and splitting it apart from what Hage calls 'critical sociology' and 'critical history.' Anthropology has a specific approach to the study of culture due to its historical development, and this toolkit of knowledge production would be wasted if it is not used to study every aspect of society. In conclusion, focusing on a 'primitivist anthropology' risks curtailing the discipline and reinforcing its boundaries as a study of only the fictional 'other' and not of ourselves. As Clifford writes, "The relations of power whereby one portion of humanity can select, value and collect the pure products of others needs to be criticized and transformed" (1988: 213).
