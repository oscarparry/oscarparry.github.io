Why the study of households matters for an analysis of the economy

16 May 2023 EconomicsAnthropology

Written for AN456, Anthropology of Economy. The household is presented as a natural unit standing outside the economy. It is neither natural nor outside, and is presented as such to allow for capital accumulation.

Set question: Why is the study of households important for an analysis of the economy?

In this essay I will argue that the household is important to the analysis of the economy precisely because it is defined as lying outside it. The image of the household as a private, natural unit, held together by kinship rather than by exchange, is not a description of how people actually live. It is an ideological construction with a specific European history, allowing the unpaid labour of reproducing the workforce to be invisible in the accounting of the economy. I will trace the critique of the 'natural household' from Engels through Whitehead, Strathern and Harris, show through ethnography that real households almost never conform to the model (Li, Stack, Koch, Mies), and end on the political question of what follows from this, where I will argue with Davis against the wages for housework position.

The critique of the household as a natural unit has a long lineage. Engels' The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State, drawing heavily on Lewis Henry Morgan's Ancient Society, tied the modern family to the emergence of private property. Whitehead has observed that these classic discussions of the relationship between personhood and property were founded on evolutionary presumptions and relied on a dichotomous contrast between types of society. Marx's analysis of commodity fetishism hinges on the distinction between a Western concept of property which embodies the legal separation of subject and object, and its historical opposite, in which these characteristics are reversed. A similar dichotomy underlies Mauss's distinction between archaic gift societies and modern commodity societies in which people transact freely with one another and objects are alienated commodities, separate from the giver and the recipient. The problem with this framing is that it takes the categories produced by capitalist society and reads them backwards into every other social arrangement.

Strathern makes the point sharply. It is bourgeois political thought in capitalist society that sets up as arbitrary the relation between persons on the one hand and, on the other, the things that come to stand for persons' performances and statuses. The paradigm is labour power, abstracted from the person and acquiring an independent value. Once that abstraction is in place, 'the kinship system' and 'the economic system' assume the character of bounded entities, because their internal systemic nature gives them an apparently natural coherence. The separation is not discovered in the world; it is produced by a particular way of thinking about the world, and then found everywhere.

Harris shows how thoroughly this construction is gendered. The sense that women are natural beings while men are able to transcend nature and become fully cultural is deeply embedded in European thought. Nature as a concept is itself a product of particular cultures, and ideas about what is natural, and the values assigned to it, vary correspondingly (Strathern). The definitions of motherhood, childhood and fatherhood, and the representation of the home as a haven in a heartless world, have been forged out of ideological and legal campaigns, and are subject to constant renegotiation as needs and circumstances change. They are the outcome of struggle, not the starting conditions of social life.

The assumption of a self-sufficient household underpins a substantial body of economic anthropology. Sahlins' 'domestic mode of production' draws on Chayanov, who argued against the prevailing Leninist orthodoxy of his time that the peasant economy was not based on the same calculations as a capitalist enterprise, but was oriented to the consumption needs of the household, exploiting its own labour until the needs of all its members were satisfied and no more. Fundamental to such theories is the assumption that the household or family transcends both historical and social forces. Starting from assumptions about the discontinuity in forms of exchange within and between households under capitalism, it is easy to read backwards to non-capitalist economic systems and see the same discontinuity there.

Harris identifies what this assumption misses. Chayanov's theory in fact depends on something scarcely mentioned in it: the market. In the South Russian peasantry at the turn of the century there was a market for land, labour and produce, and this set the parameters for peasant economic behaviour. Households have only the appearance of autonomy, since relations with other households are mediated through the abstract form of money. It is precisely because relations between peasant households were vitally affected by commodity exchange that the household appeared so distinct and so apparently based on a unique structure of non-commodity relations. The separation is an effect of the market, not an alternative to it.

The consequences of taking the separation at face value are serious. Either society is construed as a series of identical units held together in some ill-defined way, which is possibly what Marx had in mind when describing the French peasantry of 1851 as a 'sack of potatoes', or society is seen as standing outside or in opposition to the household, which underlies the whole distinction between domestic and public spheres. The state is deeply implicated here. Many aspects of male power over other household members derive from the nature of the state: household heads pay taxes, negotiate contracts, make sharecropping arrangements and lease land, and thereby exercise control over the lives of wives, children and dependent kin. The very activity of census taking, fundamental to state organisation, defines households precisely around the identification of a single person answerable for the other members. The constitution of a domain apparently separate from the public world is the means by which women are effectively controlled. The more organisationally separate households are, the more women are confined within domestic space, and the more total is their dependence on the men who represent them in the world at large.

The ethnography shows how badly the 'natural household' model fits reality. Li's Lauje highlanders reorganise their conception of the household when capitalist relations of private property arrive. Where land was previously held in common between larger kinship groups, each individual household comes to be organised around a smaller unit, often with a patriarchal head who can deal with the state to arrange loans and contracts. This is a striking example of what Scott calls legibility, the reorganisation of social life to conform to state-mandated models in order to gain access to the benefits of a capitalist system. Stack's work in The Flats shows the opposite movement. Responsibility for providing food, care, clothing and shelter, and for socialising children, is spread across several households within domestic networks. Which household a given individual belongs to is not a particularly meaningful question, because daily domestic organisation depends on where people sleep, where they eat, and where they offer their time and money. Household changes rarely affect the exchanges and daily dependencies of those who take part in common activity.

Koch shows the ideal household doing disciplinary work precisely because so few people conform to it. The model is used against those who fall outside it, most visibly single mothers on benefits on a council estate. The household does not have to describe anyone accurately in order to function as a standard against which people are found wanting.

Mies gives the clearest case of what the separation conceals. In the Narsapur lace industry, the organisation of production as a household industry and the social definition of the lace workers as housewives rest on a specific interconnection between production and reproduction which in other industries is usually separated. It is exactly the non-separation of these spheres that forms the precondition for the exploitation of the lace makers. This sector is normally categorised as unorganised or informal and treated as not yet fully subsumed under capitalist production relations, sometimes as a transitional or pre-capitalist mode of production. A concrete analysis of the actual relations between the groups involved shows those conceptualisations to be inadequate, because they cannot penetrate the mystification created by the separation of production and reproduction. The women themselves conceived of their work as supplementary to their husbands', and as requiring no special skill, explaining the gap between male and female wages by the fact that they were doing unskilled work. They had internalised the idea that their work was not work in the real sense. Mies also notes the cracks in this: the same women could not ignore that the untouchable Mala and Madiga women they looked down on earned more precisely because they were not respectable housewives but workers, and when asked whether they would be willing to work together in one place, they said they would.

What follows politically is contested. Federici's position, developed from Caliban and the Witch, treats the subordination of women into the household in the transition to capitalism as constitutive of the system rather than incidental to it, and is wary of socialised provision on the grounds that state childcare can become a vehicle for shaping children in the state's interests. Davis takes the harder line and I think the better one. Freed from its exclusive affiliation with the female sex, housework would not thereby cease to be oppressive; neither women nor men should waste precious hours of their lives on work that is neither stimulating, creative nor productive. A substantial portion of domestic work could be incorporated into the industrial economy: teams of trained and well-paid workers, using advanced equipment, could accomplish swiftly and efficiently what the present-day houseworker does arduously and alone. The reason this potential goes unmentioned is that the capitalist economy is structurally hostile to the industrialisation of housework, because socialised housework implies large public subsidy to reach the working-class families who need it most, and little in the way of profit would result.

This is why I think the wages for housework position is a dead end. It assumes that if women were paid for being housewives they would accordingly enjoy a higher social status, when the age-old struggles of the paid household worker, whose condition is more miserable than that of almost any other group of workers under capitalism, tell a different story. Dalla Costa advocates the housewives' strike, the rejection of the home as itself a form of struggle. But if women leave the home, where do they go, and how do they unite with other women? It is more realistic to call on women to leave home for outside jobs, or at least to participate in a mass campaign for decent jobs for women, because it is on the job that women can unite with their sisters and their brothers and challenge the capitalists at the point of production. Mies' lace makers make the argument concrete: even when women are engaged in commodity production, confinement to the domestic sphere prevents them from organising effectively to fight for better wages. The atomisation is the point.

In conclusion, the study of households matters for the analysis of the economy because the boundary between them is where the ideological work of capitalism is done. Defining the household as natural, private and outside the economy allows the cost of reproducing the labour force to be carried without being counted, and it disorganises the very people who bear that cost. The categories are not descriptions of social life but instruments in it, and any economic analysis that accepts them at face value will reproduce the mystification rather than explain it.
